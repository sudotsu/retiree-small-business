# Lesson 2.1 — The Elements of Your Big Picture

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 30 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

> **Note:** This is a full-inventory lesson. The people, activities, and roles exercise takes longer than it looks on first read. Allow the full 75 minutes and do not rush the list — completeness matters more than speed here.

---

## Lesson Overview

Last week you looked inward. You mapped your decision drivers, your default settings, the attitudes and beliefs that run quietly in the background of everything you do.

This week you look outward.

The "big picture" is everything that makes up your life as it actually exists right now — the people in it, the things you do, and the roles you play every day. Before you can build a business that fits your life, you need to see your life clearly as a whole. Not just the parts you're proud of or the parts that are easy to name, but the complete picture.

This lesson gives you a structured way to do that.

By the end of Lesson 2.1, you will have:

- Identified every significant person currently in your big picture, including those who support you and those who cost you energy.
- Listed the activities that define how you actually spend your time.
- Named the roles through which other people see and know you.
- Begun to see where your business fits — and where it does not yet appear.

## Part 1 — The Picasso Principle: Why the Whole Picture Comes First

Pablo Picasso, toward the end of his life, described the way artists sometimes get lost in a single detail. He used a peach as an example. A painter might spend hours making sure one peach, a small element in the lower corner of a canvas, is perfectly rendered — the blush of color exactly right, the shadow just so. And it might be a beautiful peach.

But most people who look at the painting will never notice it.

What they see is the whole picture. And if the whole picture doesn't work, the peach doesn't matter.

There is a lesson in that for everyone starting a business in this chapter of life.

Your business is the peach. It needs to be well made. But it lives inside a larger canvas — your life. If you work on the business without stepping back to see the full picture first, you risk building something that, however good it is on its own, doesn't fit the life you actually want to live.

The big picture, for our purposes, means this: **all the things that make up your life, and how they relate to each other.**

That is a deliberately broad definition because life is broad. It includes the people you love and the people who exhaust you. It includes the things you do out of joy and the things you do out of obligation. It includes the identity you carry at home, the one you carry in your community, and the one you are beginning to build in business.

You may be wondering: what is the difference between the big picture and the default settings you worked on in Module 1?

Here is the distinction. Your default settings — your attitudes, beliefs, motivations, and the people who shape your inner life — are mostly **internal and invisible.** Other people cannot see them directly. They can only see their effects.

Your big picture elements are mostly **external and visible.** They are the facts of your life as it appears to the world. They can be observed, listed, drawn, and reorganized.

One lives inside you. The other surrounds you. You need both maps to build something that lasts.

## Part 2 — The Three Pillars of Your Big Picture

Every big picture is built from the same three kinds of elements. Understanding what belongs in each category before you start listing will make your map more accurate and more useful.

### People

People are the most important element. They belong in your big picture whether their influence on you is positive or negative. This is not the time to be polite or selective.

Some people will be individuals: your spouse, your adult children, a close friend, a doctor you trust, a neighbor you rely on. Others will be groups: your faith community, a civic club, a local veterans' group, a neighborhood association, a circle of old colleagues.

Include the people who lift you. Also include the people who drain you — those who consume your time without return, who undermine your confidence without realizing it, or who see your business idea as a phase rather than a plan. They belong in the picture because they are in your life, and ignoring them does not make their influence disappear. Knowing they are there helps you plan for them.

### Activities

Activities are what you actually do — not what you think you should do, and not what you used to do. What fills your days and weeks right now?

This can include things you do at home (cooking, maintaining the house, caring for a grandchild), things you do in your community (volunteering, attending church, taking a class), and things you do for yourself (walking, reading, gardening, a hobby you have kept for years). It also includes work you may still be doing in a part-time or consulting capacity.

Be honest here. If you spend two hours a day watching the news, that is an activity. If you drive your sister to her medical appointments every week, that is an activity. These are not flaws — they are facts, and your business will need to fit around them.

### Roles

Roles are how other people see and relate to you. They are the identities you carry in different contexts.

Some roles come from family: parent, grandparent, sibling, spouse. Some come from activities: volunteer, caregiver, committee member, coach, mentor. Some come from the community: neighbor, deacon, board member.

And some — perhaps not yet, but soon — come from business: entrepreneur, business owner, service provider, consultant.

For today's exercise, try to include at least two roles from each area: family, activities, and community or work. Aim for seven total.

## Part 3 — What Your Map Is Telling You

Before you move to Exercise 2, take two minutes to simply look at what you have written.

A few questions worth sitting with:

**On your people list:** Did you include anyone whose presence in your life costs more than it gives? Most people hesitate to list those names. But they belong there, because your business will have to compete with the time and energy those relationships require. You cannot plan around what you have not acknowledged.

**On your activities list:** Is there anything on the list that you do purely out of inertia — because you have always done it, not because it still matters to you? There may be room in your schedule that is currently occupied, not because it is needed, but because no one has questioned it yet.

**On your roles list:** Look at the last role you wrote. Did "business owner" appear anywhere — even in a tentative form? If it did not, that is worth noticing. It is not a problem; many people beginning this course have not yet claimed that identity. But the moment you write it down, something shifts. You are not just a retiree thinking about a business. You are a business owner who has not started yet. There is a difference in how you carry yourself.

## Exercise 2 — Weight Your Elements

Now that your lists are built, identify the most central elements in your picture right now.

**Step 1:** Go back through each list and circle three items in each category — the three people, the three activities, and the three roles that feel most present and most significant in your life at this moment.

**Step 2:** Put a small checkmark next to any item in any list that feels out of balance — either taking more space than it should, or receiving less attention than it deserves.

**Step 3:** In the space below, write one sentence about what your circled and marked items, taken together, tell you about your life right now.

> My big picture right now is primarily defined by \_\_\_\_, and the area that feels most out of balance is \_\_\_\_.

This sentence is not a judgment. It is a starting point.

## Mini-Story — Frank's Overlooked Picture

Frank retired from thirty-one years in insurance and immediately threw himself into plans for a consulting practice. He had clients lined up, a business card designed, and a logo chosen.

Six months in, he was frustrated and exhausted, and he could not figure out why. He had everything planned out.

What Frank had not done was step back to look at the full canvas. He had not listed the fact that he was the primary caregiver for his mother-in-law three mornings a week. He had not accounted for the fact that his closest friendships were built around a Wednesday morning coffee group — which he had stopped attending because he was "too busy with the business." He had not noticed that he had quietly removed himself from his two most energizing roles — mentor and grandfather — in order to chase a role, consultant, that was not yet real.

Frank came back to the big picture exercise. He rebuilt his list honestly. And then he did something that changed everything: he put "grandfather" and "mentor" back at the center of the picture, and designed his consulting work to fit around them rather than displace them.

Within a year, his practice was running and his relationships were intact. Not because he worked harder, but because he worked from the right picture.

## Journal Prompts

Take 15 minutes to write full-sentence answers to the following questions. Do not summarize. Write out your actual thoughts, even if they surprise you.

1. Looking at your people list, which person or group did you almost leave off — and why? What does that tell you about how much space they actually take in your life?

2. Is there an activity on your list that feels like obligation rather than choice? If you removed it, what would change — and what has kept you from doing that?

3. When you look at your roles list, which role do you feel most confident in right now? Which role do you feel least settled in — and is "business owner" anywhere in that picture?

4. What does your big picture map reveal about what your business will need to work around or with? Be specific — name the people, activities, or roles that a business would have to fit alongside.

## Transition to Lesson 2.2 — Flavors, Colors, and Sounds

You now have a written inventory of your big picture: the people, activities, and roles that make up your life as it actually is.

But a list is still flat. It tells you what is there. It does not yet tell you what it feels like — whether your picture is vibrant or muted, energized or exhausted, alive with direction or quietly drifting.

In Lesson 2.2, you will add a second dimension to your big picture by describing it in sensory terms. Flavors. Colors. Sounds. This is not an art exercise. It is a tool for seeing your life more honestly than a list of words allows — and for articulating what you want it to become.
