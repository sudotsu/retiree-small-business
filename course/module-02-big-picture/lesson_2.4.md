## Lesson Overview

You have spent three days building pictures. Your life's big picture. The sensory gap between where you are and where you want to be. Your business picture in two versions — present and desired.

Today that picture work pays off.

This lesson is where the week shifts from reflection to action. You will write five behavioral objectives for your business — specific, structured statements that describe what you want to change, exactly how you will change it, and precisely who is responsible.

This is not busywork. It is the difference between a vision and a plan. A vision describes where you want to go. An objective describes a specific vehicle, a specific route, and a specific person behind the wheel. You need both. But without the objective, the vision stays a picture.

## Part 1 — Why Most Objectives Fail

If you have ever sat in a business meeting — or a committee, a board room, a church council, a school planning session — you have very likely witnessed the production of objectives that were not really objectives at all.

They sound like objectives. They use words like "improve," "enhance," "grow," and "focus." They get written on a whiteboard, photographed, and emailed around afterward as though something meaningful has been decided. But they do not direct behavior. They do not assign responsibility. They cannot be measured. And because they cannot be measured, no one ever has a real conversation about whether they were fulfilled.

The people in those rooms are not lazy or careless. The format they are using is simply incomplete.

Here is what an incomplete objective looks like:

> "Our objective is to grow our customer base."

That statement identifies a direction. It does not say by how much, through what specific behavior, or by whom. Six months from now, if you have two more customers than you did today, did you fulfill it? Did you fall short of it? There is no way to tell, so the question is never asked.

Here is what a complete objective looks like:

> "My business will add fifteen new clients over the next six months through a personal referral program in which I ask each of my current clients, one at a time, to introduce me to one person they believe would benefit from my service — with a goal of converting at least one new paid client per month."

Now you can have an honest conversation in six months. Did you do it? What happened? Where did it work and where did it break down?

The difference between those two statements is not sophistication — it is structure. The second one uses a formula that anyone can apply once they know it.

## Part 2 — The Three-Part Formula

Every effective behavioral objective contains three elements. They can appear in any order within the sentence, but all three must be present.

**Element 1: A Specific Target**

This is the outcome you are aiming for, stated in a form that can be verified. "More customers" is not a specific target. "Fifteen new clients in six months" is. "My first $500 in revenue" is. "One paid engagement before the end of next month" is.

The target answers the question: *How will I know, unambiguously, when this objective has been met?*

If you cannot answer that question, the target is not specific enough yet.

**Element 2: A Specific Behavior**

This is the action — the actual work — that will produce the target. Not the outcome, but the mechanism. "I will reach out to people" is not a specific behavior. "I will personally call each of my eight former colleagues and offer a thirty-minute no-cost conversation about what I am building, with the goal of asking each one for a single referral" is a specific behavior.

The behavior answers the question: *What will I actually do, in concrete terms, to make this happen?*

The more precisely you define the behavior, the more control you have over whether the objective moves forward.

**Element 3: Specific People**

This is who is responsible for the behavior. In the early stages of a small business, the answer will almost always be "I will" — but saying it explicitly matters more than it seems. It removes the passive voice, the comfortable assumption that something will happen on its own, and the organizational fog that allows accountability to evaporate.

If you plan to involve anyone else — a partner, a family member, someone helping you — name them. "My son David and I will both post three times per week" is far more accountable than "we will post regularly."

The people element answers the question: *Who is specifically responsible for carrying out this behavior?*

## Part 3 — From Big Picture to Objectives

Look back at your business big picture from Lesson 2.3. Specifically, look at the two or three most significant differences you identified between your "as it is today" picture and your "as I want it to be" picture.

Each of those differences is a candidate for an objective. The difference tells you what needs to change. The objective specifies how it will change, by how much, and who is responsible.

A few examples of how a big picture gap becomes an objective:

**Gap identified:** "I want customers, but right now I have none."

**Objective:** "I will secure my first three paying clients within sixty days by personally contacting twenty people from my existing network — former colleagues, neighbors, and members of my church — and offering each one a free thirty-minute introductory session, with the goal of converting three into paid engagements."

---

**Gap identified:** "I want my business to feel organized, but right now it feels scattered."

**Objective:** "I will establish a consistent weekly work routine within the next two weeks by scheduling ninety-minute work blocks from 9:00 to 10:30 a.m., Monday through Friday, each with a specific assigned focus — Monday for planning, Tuesday and Wednesday for client work, Thursday for outreach, Friday for review."

---

**Gap identified:** "I want people in my community to know my business exists, but right now no one does."

**Objective:** "I will introduce my business to at least thirty people in my community over the next sixty days by attending three local events — two farmers markets and one neighborhood association meeting — bringing printed business cards and a prepared one-sentence description of what I offer, and following up with anyone who expresses interest within forty-eight hours."

---

These are examples only. Your five objectives should come directly from your own big picture gaps, in your own language, using targets and behaviors that reflect your actual situation and capacity right now.

## Mini-Story — Raymond and the Objective That Finally Worked

Raymond had spent twenty-six years in municipal government. He retired knowing exactly what he wanted to do: help small nonprofits and community organizations with grant writing, which he had done informally within his department for years, without anyone ever officially asking him to.

His first attempt at an objective was this: "I want to help more nonprofits get funding."

He looked at it for a long time. It felt right. It was true and it was meaningful. It described exactly what he cared about.

A friend in his course cohort read it and asked, simply: "How will you know when you've done it?"

Raymond did not have an answer.

He tried again: "I will secure two paid grant writing contracts within ninety days by personally reaching out to eight nonprofit directors — three former colleagues and five organizations I have followed for years — and offering each one a no-cost grant readiness assessment, with the goal of converting at least two into paid contracts at $800 each."

He got his first contract in forty-three days.

Not because he worked harder the second time. But because he knew precisely what he was working toward, exactly what he needed to do to get there, and there was no ambiguity about who was responsible.

The formula is not magic. It is just complete.

## Journal Prompts

Take 15 minutes. Write full sentences — not bullet points, not fragments. Complete thoughts.

1. Look at your five objectives. Which one feels most urgent to you, and which feels most like a stretch beyond your current comfort? What does that difference tell you about where your confidence and your hesitation each live right now?

2. Think about the behaviors you specified in your five objectives. Are these behaviors you already know how to execute, or are some of them things you will need to learn or figure out? For the ones that require learning, what is your plan?

3. If you fulfilled all five of these objectives, walk through your "as I want it to be" business picture from Lesson 2.3 and describe in concrete terms what would look different. Be specific.

4. Which one of your five objectives, if it were the only one you accomplished this year, would make the most meaningful difference to your business and your life? Why that one?

## Community Prompt

Post one of your five objectives in the community group — your choice of which one:

> "Here is one of my five objectives: \_\_\_\_. The element I found hardest to make specific was \_\_\_\_."

Then read two other members' objectives and give them feedback using only the three-element structure. Does the objective have a specific target? A specific behavior? Specific people named? Tell them what you see — honestly and helpfully.

This kind of peer review is what most business planning skips. It matters. What you notice in someone else's objective is often the same thing you need to sharpen in your own.

## Transition to Lesson 2.5 — Due Dates: Making Good Objectives Great

You have five solid objectives. They are specific, behavioral, and accountable. They are meaningfully better than the majority of planning statements produced in most professional settings.

But they are not yet great. One element is still missing — and without it, even a well-written objective will drift.

That element is a due date.

Not every objective deserves the same deadline. Some are urgent and ready to begin immediately. Some are important but depend on preparation that has not happened yet. Assigning due dates thoughtlessly — giving everything the same deadline because it feels decisive — is nearly as useless as having no due dates at all.

In Lesson 2.5, you will evaluate your five objectives for both importance and readiness, assign realistic and differentiated due dates to each, and produce the final complete versions that will guide your business activity through the weeks ahead.
