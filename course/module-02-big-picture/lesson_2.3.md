# Lesson 2.3 — The Big Picture for Your Business

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

## Lesson Overview

Over the past two days you have built a clear picture of your life as a whole: the people, activities, and roles that fill it, and the sensory qualities that describe it honestly. You have identified the gap between where your life is and where you want it to be.

Today you zoom in.

You will apply the exact same framework — people, activities, roles, flavors, colors, and sounds — to your business specifically. Not as a separate exercise, but as a picture that lives inside the larger canvas you have already built.

This distinction is not a formality. It is the most important organizing principle of this entire module: your business belongs inside your life's big picture. Your life does not belong inside your business.

That sounds obvious. It is not, in practice, easy to maintain. This lesson will help you build it correctly from the beginning.

By the end of Lesson 2.3, you will have:

- Applied the full big picture framework to your business — both as it exists today and as you want it to be.
- Identified the specific people, activities, and roles that will make up your business life.
- Noted the flavors, colors, and sounds you want your business to carry — and whether they align with what you said you wanted in your life overall.
- Identified the most important positive changes to build toward, which will become the foundation for your objectives in Lessons 2.4 and 2.5.

## Part 1 — Business Inside Life, Not Life Inside Business

There is a version of entrepreneurship that devours the entrepreneur.

It is not dramatic — it does not happen all at once. It happens gradually. First the business takes the mornings, because mornings are quiet and productive. Then it takes the evenings, because there is always something unfinished. Then it takes the weekends, because that is when you can finally think. Then one day you look up and realize that the people, activities, and roles you named as most important — the grandchildren, the Tuesday morning coffee group, the walk before dinner — have been quietly replaced by the business you built to make your life better.

This is not a failure of character. It is a failure of structure. And it is entirely avoidable if you build the structure correctly at the beginning.

The rule in this course is simple: your business is one element of your life's big picture, placed within the canvas alongside your health, your family, your community, and your sense of self. It is the peach in the painting, from Lesson 2.1 — it needs to be good, but the whole canvas still needs to work.

When you build your business big picture today, you are not just planning a business. You are placing it correctly within the life you are designing. Every decision about what goes into your business — who is involved, what you do, what roles you play — should be made in light of whether it fits within the canvas without displacing everything else on it.

One practical test to carry into every business decision: *If I add this to my business picture, what does it replace in my life's big picture?* If the answer is something that belongs at the center of your life — health, a key relationship, a role you defined as essential — then the cost is too high. If the answer is a time-waster or an obligation you already identified as draining, then the trade may be exactly right.

You will fill in the picture twice: as it stands today (even if "today" is early and incomplete), and as you want it to become.

## Part 3 — Reading Your Business Gap

You now have two business pictures: today and desired. Look at the difference between them.

A few questions to work through before moving to your journal:

**Does your desired business picture fit inside your life's big picture?** Compare the "desired" column of this exercise with the big picture you described in Lessons 2.1 and 2.2. Do the people, activities, and roles in your business picture leave room for the people, activities, and roles that matter most in your life? If they do not, this is the moment to adjust — not later.

**What are the two or three most significant differences between "as it is" and "as I want it to be"?** These differences are the candidates for your business objectives in Lesson 2.4. Do not try to address everything — look for the changes that would move the picture most. Write those two or three differences in your workbook and keep them visible.

**Does your business sensory description align with your life sensory description?** In Lesson 2.2, you described the flavors, colors, and sounds you want more of in your life. Do your desired business flavor, color, and sound reinforce those — or do they pull in a different direction? If they pull in a different direction, that is worth examining before you go further.

## Mini-Story — Diane and the Picture Within the Picture

Diane retired from nursing after thirty-seven years and immediately started building what she called a "wellness consulting practice." She was energetic about it. She set up a website, printed business cards, joined three local networking groups, and started taking calls.

Within four months, she was exhausted in a way that felt familiar — the specific kind of tired that had followed her through the last ten years of her nursing career, when she had been senior enough to carry everyone else's problems.

A friend from her cohort asked her to describe her business in colors. Diane said: "The same ones as before. Hospital green and that particular shade of fluorescent white."

That was when she realized she had not built a new business. She had rebuilt the job she retired from, without the salary or the colleagues.

Diane went back to her business big picture. She looked at who was in it, what she was doing, and what role she was playing. She had replicated: caregiver, problem-solver, on-call, responsible for everyone else's outcomes.

She rewrote it. She removed the networking groups, reduced her client maximum to four per month, and added a role she had never had before in any professional setting: teacher rather than rescuer. She changed her service from "helping people solve health problems" to "teaching people the habits that prevent them."

The flavor of her business changed almost immediately. She described it as "something with herbs in it — not spicy, but alive."

The lesson: the business big picture is not just a planning tool. It is a mirror. Use it honestly.

1. When you compare your business "as it is" picture with your life's big picture from Lesson 2.1, does your business fit comfortably inside it — or is it already starting to crowd other things out? Be specific about what is being crowded.

2. What is the single most important change between your "as it is" and "as I want it to be" business pictures? Why is that change the most important one?

3. The sensory description of your desired business — does it match the life you said you wanted in Lesson 2.2? If there is a mismatch, what does it tell you?

4. Is there a person in your "as I want it to be" business picture who is not yet in your "as it is" picture? Who would that be, and what would it take to involve them?

## Transition to Lesson 2.4 — Writing Five Business Objectives

You now have two sets of pictures — your life and your business — and you can see clearly what needs to change in your business to get from where it is to where you want it to be.

Now the work gets concrete.

In Lesson 2.4, you will take the most significant differences in your business picture and convert them into five behavioral objectives — specific, measurable statements about what you will change, how you will change it, and who is responsible.

Most people who have sat in business meetings have seen objectives written badly: vague, unaccountable, and built more to satisfy a planning process than to actually direct behavior. Lesson 2.4 will show you exactly what separates a useful objective from a useless one — and help you write five of the useful kind.
