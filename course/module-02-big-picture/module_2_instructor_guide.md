# Module 2 Instructor Guide — Painting the Big Picture

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 2**

**For:** Chaz Korb (Instructor)
**Audience:** Retirees 60–75+, small cohorts or self-paced

---

## Purpose of This Guide

This guide gives you everything you need to facilitate Module 2 effectively — whether you are hosting a live cohort session, reviewing student work, responding in the community group, or recording supplemental video content. It is organized lesson by lesson with facilitation notes, common student patterns, and suggested responses.

---

## Module Overview and Instructor Framing

Module 2 is where students make the shift from self-awareness to strategy. Module 1 was internal — defaults, beliefs, decision drivers. Module 2 is the first time students look outward and begin to build something visible.

The central idea running through all five lessons is this: **the business belongs inside the life, not the other way around.** Your job as instructor is to reinforce this frame consistently. Students in this age group are especially prone to one of two failure modes:

1. **Under-commitment** — treating the big picture and objectives as theoretical exercises that don't require real follow-through.
2. **Over-commitment** — treating the business as the new career, letting it crowd out health, family, and community exactly as the old career did.

Your facilitation tone throughout the week should convey: this work is serious and real, and it is designed to serve your life — not replace it.

## Lesson-by-Lesson Notes

---

### Lesson 2.1 — The Elements of Your Big Picture

**Core concept:** People, activities, and roles make up the externally visible big picture. Defaults (Module 1) were internal. This is the first external inventory.

**Timing:** Approximately 75 minutes total. Most students will spend 30–35 minutes on the reading and 30–35 minutes on the exercises. The journal runs 10–15 minutes.

**Common student patterns to watch for:**

- **Omitting negative or draining people.** Students frequently list only supportive people. When reviewing community posts, gently prompt: "Did you include anyone in your picture who costs you energy? That matters for planning." This is not about drama — it's about honesty.
- **Listing roles by title rather than function.** "Retired manager" is a title, not a role. "Decision-maker for the household" is a function. Encourage students to describe what the role actually requires of them.
- **Not writing "business owner" as a role.** Many students at this stage have not yet claimed the identity. That's fine and expected — Lesson 2.1 surfaces it. If you respond to community posts, you might note: "Interesting that business owner doesn't appear yet — what would it take for you to write that in?"

**Good facilitation prompt for the community thread:**
> "What was the most surprising thing you found when you wrote the list out? Often the people or activities we almost left off are the ones that tell us the most."

---

### Lesson 2.2 — Flavors, Colors, and Sounds

**Core concept:** Sensory description forces specificity about both current reality and desired direction. The gap between the two is the motivational foundation for objectives.

**Timing:** ~75 minutes. The reading is more philosophical than the others in this module; some students will want to sit with it. The exercises themselves are shorter — the depth comes from the journal.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Describing desired flavors/colors/sounds that sound like marketing copy.** "Bright, energetic, and inspiring" is a brand tagline, not a personal truth. If you see this in community posts, probe gently: "What does that feel like on a Tuesday morning before coffee? What would your regular day actually smell, sound, or taste like if it were the way you want it?"
- **Skipping the gap analysis.** Some students fill in the exercise lists and move straight to writing the one-sentence big picture without sitting with the gap. Encourage them to do the comparison: which dimension is furthest from where they want it to be? That's the one worth noting.
- **The one-sentence big picture being too vague.** "A happy, purposeful life" is not useful as a guide for making business decisions. If you are doing a live review, ask: "Can you make it more specific — what does 'purposeful' look and feel like in practice for you?"

**Good community facilitation prompt:**
> "The gap between 'today' and 'desired' in this exercise is where most real motivation comes from. Did anyone find that one dimension (flavor, color, or sound) was particularly far from where you want it — and did that surprise you?"

### Lesson 2.3 — The Big Picture for Your Business

**Core concept:** Business lives inside life's big picture, not the other way around. Apply the same framework to the business specifically. The two pictures are compared, not merged.

**Timing:** ~75 minutes. The reading section in this lesson is the most important of the week — the "business inside life" principle is the single most consequential idea for this audience. Do not rush past it in video content.

**Common student patterns:**

- **The business picture consuming the life picture.** When students compare their desired business picture to their desired life picture, some will discover that the two are in conflict — the business picture requires time and energy that the life picture assigned elsewhere. This is valuable. Do not treat it as a problem to fix immediately; treat it as information worth sitting with. The next step is adjusting the business picture to fit the life, not the other way around.
- **The "as it is today" section being left blank because "I don't have a business yet."** Remind students that "as it is today" includes their current planning, thinking, and intention — not just an existing operational business. A sketch of where they are starting from is more useful than an empty page.
- **Sensory descriptions for business not matching the life picture.** This is actually one of the most important moments of the week. If a student's desired business sounds exhausting or stressful relative to the life they said they wanted, they need to see that dissonance before they write objectives. If you are reviewing work, flag it directly.

**The `<KORB STORY HERE>` note in the source material:** The original Korb text for Day 3 includes a placeholder for a personal story about a time when work consumed the big picture. This is exactly the right place for you to share your own experience — a time when professional life crowded out what mattered most. This personal story in video or live session will be the most memorable part of the week for many students. It should be specific, honest, and brief (2–3 minutes). The lesson is already written to accommodate or anticipate this story in the transition section.

### Lesson 2.4 — Writing Five Business Objectives

**Core concept:** The three-part behavioral objective formula: specific target + specific behavior + specific people. All three must be present or the objective is incomplete.

**Timing:** ~75 minutes. This is the most cognitively demanding lesson of the week. Some students will need more time on the exercises. Consider noting this in your video intro: "Give yourself the full hour today. This one is worth it."

**Common student patterns:**

- **Writing intentions instead of objectives.** "I intend to find clients by reaching out to my network" still lacks a specific target (how many clients?) and a specific behavior (how, exactly, will you reach out?). This is the most common error. The practice exercise in Exercise 1 is specifically designed to catch this before students write their own.
- **Writing five objectives that are all the same type.** Many students write five variations of "get more customers." Encourage variety: one objective might address customers, one might address routine and schedule, one might address skill development, one might address the business picture itself. Diversity of objective type creates a more complete plan.
- **Over-ambitious targets.** A student who has no current clients writing an objective to have fifty clients in thirty days is setting themselves up for discouragement. If you review objectives, apply the question: "Is this target achievable from where you are actually starting, using the behavior you described, in the time you have available?"

**Good community facilitation prompt:**
> "When you posted your objective and someone gave you feedback on the three elements, what did they catch that you had missed? The element people most commonly leave out is specific behavior — it's the hardest one to write because it requires the most honesty about what you're actually going to do."

### Lesson 2.5 — Due Dates: Making Good Objectives Great

**Core concept:** Due dates transform objectives from statements into commitments. Not all due dates are equal — assign them based on importance and readiness.

**Timing:** ~75 minutes. Students often underestimate the time needed for Exercise 1 (the priority/readiness sort) because it requires thinking, not just writing. Plan for 20–25 minutes on Exercise 1 alone.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Assigning all five objectives the same due date.** Usually thirty or sixty days from today. This is not prioritization — it is procrastination made to look like planning. Encourage students to spread due dates based on actual priority sequencing.
- **Setting due dates that are too far out.** Some students, uncomfortable with commitment, assign all their objectives six-month or twelve-month deadlines. Push back gently: "Which of these could you realistically begin acting on this week? That one probably deserves a thirty-day due date, not a six-month one."
- **Reluctance to sign.** Some students feel the signature is performative. Acknowledge that — and then note that the research on commitment devices is real: writing it down and putting your name on it meaningfully increases follow-through. It is not about performance; it is about anchoring.

**End-of-module check-in opportunity:** Module 2 is a natural moment for a short live or async check-in. Students have now produced five signed, dated objectives. Consider a brief office hours session or community prompt: "What is the one objective you are most confident about, and what is the one where you most want someone to keep you accountable?" Accountability partnerships formed around specific objectives at this stage tend to persist.

## Suggested Office Hours Topics for Week 2

- "How do I know if my business picture is realistic?"
- "My life picture and business picture conflict — what do I do?"
- "I can't think of five objectives. What if I only have two or three real ones?"
- "My due dates feel arbitrary. How do I know if they're right?"

For all of these: the answer is almost always to go back to the big picture comparison from Lesson 2.3 and look at the most significant gap. The objectives and due dates flow from that honestly.

## Video Content Notes

If you are recording a module intro video for Module 2, suggested structure (6–8 minutes):

1. Brief recap of Module 1 (the inner work is done — now we look outward).
2. The Picasso principle — the peach and the big picture. Keep this short; the lesson itself covers it well.
3. The "business inside life" principle — this is where your personal story belongs. Be honest and specific.
4. What students will produce by Friday: a big picture for their life, a big picture for their business, and five signed objectives with due dates.
5. Encourage them: "This is the first week where you build something you can actually use. Stay with it."

If you are recording lesson-specific walkthrough videos, Lessons 2.4 and 2.5 are the highest-value targets. The objective formula and the importance/readiness framework benefit significantly from a live example and a conversational explanation.

## Assessment Notes

Module 2 does not include a formal graded assessment. The deliverable is the student's five signed objectives from Lesson 2.5. When reviewing student work (if you are doing live cohort facilitation), check each objective for:

1. Specific target — can you verify whether it was met?
2. Specific behavior — is it an actual action, not an intention?
3. Specific person — is someone named?
4. Due date — is it specific (a calendar date) and realistic?

If all four are present, the objective is complete. If any are missing, that is the feedback to give.
