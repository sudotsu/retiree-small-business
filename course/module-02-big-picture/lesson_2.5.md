# Lesson 2.5 — Due Dates: Making Good Objectives Great

### Estimated Time: Approximately 80–85 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 40 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

> **Note:** The importance/readiness matrix requires you to evaluate all five objectives against two separate criteria, then assign differentiated due dates based on that evaluation. It is not a quick fill-in. Plan for the full time.

---

## Lesson Overview

You have five objectives. Each one names a specific target, a specific behavior, and a specific person responsible. If you have done the work honestly, they represent real changes that would genuinely move your business from where it is to where you want it to be.

What they do not yet have is a deadline.

That single missing element — a due date — is what separates a commitment from a wish. Without it, even a perfectly written objective can linger indefinitely in a comfortable state of "I'm planning to get to that." The date is not a threat. It is a tool. It makes time work for you instead of slipping past you.

But due dates are not all equal, and assigning them carelessly — giving everything the same deadline because it feels like action — is nearly as useless as having no dates at all. Today you will learn to evaluate each objective against two specific criteria — its importance and its readiness — and use that evaluation to assign realistic, differentiated deadlines.

By the end of Lesson 2.5, you will have:

- Understood why due dates transform objectives from statements into commitments.
- Evaluated each of your five objectives for both importance and readiness.
- Assigned a realistic, justified due date to each objective.
- Produced the final, complete versions of your five objectives — target, behavior, person, and date — that will serve as your operating guide through the weeks ahead.

---

## Part 1 — Why Due Dates Are Non-Negotiable

Consider this: how many people do you know who have been "planning to start a business" for years?

They are thoughtful people. They have ideas worth pursuing. They may have even talked through those ideas in considerable detail. But the business has not happened — not because they lack capability, but because there is no moment at which "not yet" becomes a real problem. There is no deadline. The absence of a due date means the absence of urgency, and the absence of urgency means the absence of action.

A due date makes time a tool in your hands rather than something that passes around you.

Think about it this way. Suppose you are planning a trip to visit your grandchildren three states away. You have the intention, the desire, and the general idea. Without a specific date booked, the trip stays in the category of "soon." With a date on the calendar, something shifts. You book the car or the flight. You adjust your schedule. You tell people you will be away. The intention becomes a plan, and the plan becomes an event.

Business objectives work the same way. Without a due date, your objective exists in "soon." With a due date, it exists in reality.

There is one important nuance: being realistic matters as much as being committed. A due date that is genuinely impossible — one that sets you up to fail because it does not account for how much time the work actually requires, or what else is happening in your life — is worse than no due date, because it creates the experience of failing before you have really begun. The goal is to be honest about when something can realistically be accomplished, and then to hold yourself to it.

## Part 2 — Two Criteria for Assigning Due Dates

Not all five of your objectives are equally urgent, and not all of them are equally ready to begin. Assigning the right due date to each requires thinking through two questions.

**Criterion 1: Importance**

How much does this objective move your business toward the big picture you described in Lessons 2.2 and 2.3? If you accomplished only this one objective, would it make a meaningful difference to the overall picture? Or is it more of a supporting element — useful, but not central?

High-importance objectives deserve earlier due dates. They are the changes that, if delayed, hold everything else back.

**Criterion 2: Readiness**

How prepared are you to begin acting on this objective right now? Do you have the skills, the tools, the contacts, and the time you need to begin immediately — or does this objective depend on something else happening first?

An objective you are fully ready to begin can carry a near-term due date. An objective that requires preparation first needs a date that accounts for that preparation time honestly.

The combination of these two criteria produces a natural priority order:

| Importance | Readiness | Suggested Approach |
|---|---|---|
| High | High | Start immediately. Set the earliest realistic due date. |
| High | Low | Identify what preparation is needed. Set a date that includes that preparation time. |
| Low | High | Schedule it after higher-importance work is underway. |
| Low | Low | Revisit in four to six weeks. Do not let it block progress on what matters more. |

You will use this framework in Exercise 1 to sequence your five objectives before assigning dates.

## Exercise 2 — Add Due Dates and Write Your Final Objectives

Using your priority order from Exercise 1, assign a specific due date to each objective. Write the complete final version of each objective below: target, behavior, person, and date, all together in one or two clear sentences.

A few practical guidelines before you begin:

- Use specific calendar dates, not vague phrases like "within a few months" or "by spring." A specific date creates accountability; a season does not.
- Assign your highest-priority objective the nearest realistic due date. Do not give yourself more time than you need.
- Space the remaining due dates in a way that lets each one build on the ones before it, rather than all landing at once.
- Be honest about your life. If you know that a family obligation or a health appointment will occupy a significant stretch of time in the coming weeks, account for that honestly when you set dates.

## Part 3 — What to Do With These Objectives Now

Your five final objectives are not a document to file and forget. They are a working tool. Here is how to keep them alive and useful in the weeks ahead.

**Post them where you work.** Write them on an index card or a printed sheet. Keep them on your desk, on your refrigerator, or wherever your business hour happens. Visibility is not motivational decoration — it is a daily prompt for the behavior the objective requires.

**Review them at the end of each week.** Every Friday, spend ten minutes with your objectives. For each one: are you on track? If yes, what moved it forward this week? If not, what got in the way — and is the obstacle something you can address next week, or does it signal that the due date needs to be reconsidered honestly?

**When a due date passes, evaluate — do not just reset.** If you reach a due date and the objective is not complete, do not automatically extend it by another month. First ask: did I make a real effort? If yes, what needs to change? If no, what prevented the effort — and is that obstacle still present? Honest evaluation at the deadline is where objectives become learning, and learning is what makes the next set of objectives better.

**Your objectives are allowed to evolve.** As you move through Module 3 and beyond, some objectives may become obsolete because the business has changed. Others may need to be replaced by more specific or more urgent ones. That is not failure — that is how planning works in real businesses. Keep your objectives current and they will stay useful.

## Mini-Story — Margaret and the Calendar She Finally Used

Margaret had three years of journal entries about the gift basket business she wanted to start. Beautiful entries — thoughtful, specific, full of real ideas. She had described her ideal customer, sketched a pricing structure, and written out her three favorite product combinations in considerable detail.

She had no due dates anywhere.

When a friend asked when she was planning to start, Margaret said, "Soon." When her friend pressed — "What does soon mean? June? September? By your birthday?" — Margaret did not have an answer.

Her friend handed her a paper calendar and said: "Put a date on the first thing you would need to do. One date. That's it."

Margaret wrote, on the following Thursday: "Order sample supplies: $40." She circled it.

She ordered the supplies on Thursday.

Not because the calendar was magic. But because a circle on a Thursday is a different kind of thing than "soon." It belongs to a specific day in the real world. And when that day came, there was a circle waiting.

Her business launched fourteen weeks later. She had seven regular clients before Christmas.

The business was in the journal the whole time. The calendar was what moved it off the page.

## Journal Prompts

Take 15 minutes. Write complete sentences.

1. Look at the due date you assigned to your highest-priority objective. Is it genuinely the nearest realistic date — or did you give yourself extra time as a cushion against the discomfort of committing? Be honest about which it is, and why.

2. Which of your five due dates feels most uncertain — the one you are least confident you set correctly? What would need to be true for you to feel certain about it?

3. Think about the weeks and months between now and your furthest due date. What is the most significant thing that could get in the way — not as an excuse, but as a genuine obstacle worth planning for? What would your response be if it materialized?

4. You have now spent five days building a big picture for your life, a big picture for your business, and five dated objectives that connect the two. What is the most useful thing you built this week — not the most impressive, but the most genuinely useful to you going forward?
