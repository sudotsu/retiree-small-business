# Lesson 2.2 — Flavors, Colors, and Sounds: Seeing Your Life in Three Dimensions

### Estimated Time: Approximately 70–75 minutes

*(Reading: 25 minutes | Exercises: 30 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

In Lesson 2.1 you built an inventory: the people, activities, and roles that make up your life. That list is honest and useful, and you should keep it close.

But a list is still flat.

Words on a page describe what is in your life. They do not capture what it feels like to live it — whether it is energizing or draining, rich with direction or quietly hollowed out by routine. And they do not capture, with enough vividness, what you actually want it to become.

This lesson adds a second dimension to your big picture by asking you to describe it in sensory terms: flavors, colors, and sounds. If that sounds unusual for a business course, you are right — it is unusual. It is also one of the most effective tools in this entire program, for reasons that will become clear as you work through it.

By the end of Lesson 2.2, you will have:

- Described your big picture as it exists right now in sensory terms — with honesty, not performance.
- Described your big picture as you want it to be in sensory terms — with specificity, not wishful thinking.
- Identified the gap between those two pictures, which is the raw material for every objective you write this week.
- Written a single sentence that captures your big picture as you want it to become.

---

## Part 1 — Why Sensory Language Reveals What Lists Miss

Large organizations pay significant amounts of money to consultants who help them do exactly what this lesson asks you to do: describe their situation and their aspirations in terms that go beyond facts and figures.

Why? Because facts and figures describe a situation. They do not ignite commitment to changing it.

There is a difference between knowing that your current life is "busy and unfocused" and being able to say that it tastes like burnt coffee — functional, but without pleasure, consumed out of habit more than enjoyment. The second description lands differently. It is specific, personal, and hard to ignore. It creates dissatisfaction with the status quo in a way that a checklist item cannot.

The same is true of aspiration. "I want a better life" is nearly useless as a motivating statement. "I want my life to feel like a Saturday morning in September — unhurried, purposeful, with good light and the smell of something worth doing" is something you can feel. It gives you something to move toward.

The goal of this lesson is not poetry. The goal is precision. Sensory descriptions force you to be specific about what you have and what you want in a way that ordinary language often sidesteps.

There are three dimensions:

**Flavor** — what taste does your life leave? In your mouth, and in the experience of the people around you? Is it satisfying, bland, bitter, oversweet? Does it linger or disappear quickly?

**Color** — what do you see when you look at your life? Are the colors bright or muted? Natural or artificial? Do the colors of your daily experience reflect who you are, or who you used to be?

**Sound** — what is playing in the background of your life? Music or noise? Calm or constant interruption? When you think about your best days, what do they sound like?

You will answer each of these questions twice: once for your life as it is right now, and once for the life you are working toward. The gap between those two answers is your motivation — real, personal, and specific to you.

## Part 2 — Reading the Gap

With your answers written out, you now have two pictures: the one you live in today, and the one you want to inhabit.

Look at the distance between them. This is not a failure. This is information. The gap between where you are and where you want to be is what makes a goal worth pursuing. If the two pictures were identical, there would be nothing to build.

Notice a few things:

**Which dimension has the largest gap?** Flavor, color, or sound — which is furthest from where you want it to be? That dimension tends to point toward the area of your life most ready for change.

**Which desired element is already partly present?** If one of your "want to add" colors or sounds is already showing up on good days, you are closer than you think. You are not starting from zero — you are amplifying something already real.

**Is your business in the gap?** Look at your "today" picture and your "want" picture. Somewhere in that difference, there is likely room for a business — not just as an income source, but as a source of the flavor, color, and sound you said you want more of. Keep that connection visible as the week continues.

## Mini-Story — Gloria's Colors

Gloria spent thirty-four years as a social worker. When she retired, she described her retirement in practical terms: quiet, restful, free. And it was all of those things, for about eight months.

Then a friend asked her to describe her days in three colors. She thought for a moment and said: gray, beige, and what she called "waiting room white."

She was startled by her own answer.

The colors she said she wanted were entirely different: deep green, warm amber, something she called "brick red — the color of something solid being built." She had no idea where that imagery came from. But the moment she said it, she knew exactly what it meant.

Gloria had spent her career building things for other people. She had been enormously good at it. And now, without a structure to build within, she felt colorless.

She went back to her big picture list and looked at her roles. She had written: grandmother, volunteer, neighbor, retired. She had not written anything that resembled the person who had driven forty-five minutes each way to a job she chose because she believed it mattered.

She added one role, tentatively: builder.

Within three months, Gloria had launched a small service helping families navigate elder care paperwork — exactly the kind of thing she had helped families with for decades, now on her own terms, on her own schedule, for her own reasons.

The business came from the gap between gray and brick red. She just had to see it first.

## Journal Prompts

Take 15 minutes with these. Write full sentences. Let yourself be surprised by what comes out.

1. When you look at the gap between your "today" and "desired" flavors, colors, and sounds, what is the single most significant difference? What do you think is keeping that difference in place?

2. Which of your desired sensory elements — a flavor, color, or sound you want more of — could a business actually provide? Be specific about how.

3. Your one-sentence big picture description: what does it reveal about what you value that your current life does not fully reflect?

4. Is there a role from your Lesson 2.1 list that appears in your "desired" picture but not in your "today" picture? What would it take to add it?

## Transition to Lesson 2.3 — The Big Picture for Your Business

You now have two versions of your life's big picture: as it is, and as you want it to be. And you have a one-sentence description of the life you are designing toward.

In Lesson 2.3, you will zoom in.

Using the exact same framework — people, activities, roles, flavors, colors, sounds — you will build the big picture for your business specifically. Not your life as a whole, but the business that lives within it.

This is where the week begins to shift from self-awareness to strategy.

One critical thing to remember before you start: your business is part of your life's big picture. It is not the whole of it. If you place your health, family, and personal joy inside the framework of your business rather than the other way around, you will build something that costs more than it gives. That is a mistake worth making once, and worth avoiding if you can.

Lesson 2.3 is designed to help you avoid it.
