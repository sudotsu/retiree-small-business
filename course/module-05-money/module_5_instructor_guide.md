# Module 5 Instructor Guide — The Art of Making Money

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 5**

**For:** Chaz Korb (Instructor)

---

## Module Overview and Instructor Framing

Module 5 is the first week of the course that requires students to work with real financial data — their own numbers, pulled from actual bank statements and records. That is a different kind of vulnerability than Module 1's self-reflection or Module 4's marketing draft. Numbers are specific. They do not allow vagueness, and for many students they carry a weight that opinions and plans do not.

The framing that matters most this week: financial clarity is not a judgment, it is a tool. A budget that shows a thin margin or a cash flow gap is not evidence that the student is failing. It is evidence that the tool is working — that the picture is becoming visible enough to act on. Students who come to office hours worried about their numbers almost always have a solvable problem, not a fatal one. The most useful thing Chaz can do is separate the anxiety from the data and treat the data as information.

The Andy Warhol frame from the source material — "making money is art, and good business is the best art" — is worth repeating in any welcome video or office hours opening. It repositions the financial discipline as craft rather than compliance.

## Lesson-by-Lesson Notes

---

### Lesson 5.1 — Revenues, Expenses, and Your First Budget Picture

**Core concept:** Without a financial picture, a business can be busy, respected, and still go broke. The budget provides that picture. Revenue, fixed expenses, and variable expenses are the three categories that define the picture.

**Timing:** ~85–90 minutes. The three-month worksheet takes significantly longer than students expect, particularly for those who do not have organized records. Students should have their bank statements and receipts available before starting. If a student does not have three months of actual data (newer businesses), estimates are acceptable — the lesson notes this explicitly.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who skip to estimates without looking at actuals.** The exercise specifically asks for actual data where available. Students who estimate everything when they have records are usually avoiding the discomfort of seeing the real numbers. Gently: "Run the actual numbers first. If something surprises you, that's the point. Then we can look at what to do about it."

- **Students who cannot distinguish fixed from variable.** Some expenses genuinely sit between categories — a subscription that scales with usage, a part-time helper whose hours vary. The lesson's Exercise 2 directly addresses this. The right answer for ambiguous expenses is usually: fixed if it would exist regardless of business volume, variable if it would drop to zero if the business stopped.

- **Students who arrive with no organized records.** This is more common in older cohorts. The best response is practical: "Spend the first thirty minutes just pulling your bank statement. We don't need anything fancy — just the deposits and major payments line by line." The lesson's exercise accommodates this. The workbook has the tables already formatted.

---

### Lesson 5.2 — Spotting Trends: Reading the Signals in Your Business

**Core concept:** Trends — seasonal, demographic, behavioral — are already present in a business before the owner has named them. Naming them converts them from surprises into planned events.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The written categories in Exercise 1 take more time for some students than the format implies, particularly if they have not observed their business this way before. Allow extra time for the ranking exercise.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who say "my business doesn't have trends."** Every business has patterns — they simply have not been observed yet. A useful prompt: "Think about last year. Was there any month that was noticeably busier than the others? Any month that was slow? That pattern is a trend whether or not you planned for it."

- **Students who identify only positive trends.** The trend spotting exercise includes growth areas in revenues and growth areas in expenses. Students who focus only on revenue growth without noting cost growth will produce a budget in Lesson 5.5 that is systematically optimistic on the expense side.

---

### Lesson 5.3 — Predicting Outcomes: Looking Three Days Ahead

**Core concept:** Useful prediction is not guessing — it is identifying what is already in motion and building conservative, specific planning assumptions from it. Probable changes go into the budget. Possible changes go on a watch list.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The probable/possible classification in Exercise 2 is where students often stall. Help them with the test: "Is this already in motion — a client who has told you something, an expense you have already committed to, a change in your community you can see beginning — or is it something that might happen?"

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who predict big revenue jumps.** If a student says they plan to double their client base in Q2, the right response is: "What would you budget for if you added only half of what you are expecting? Let's build the conservative version." Optimistic predictions on the revenue side are the most common reason working budgets fail to match reality.

- **Students who predict very little.** Some students are cautious enough that they decline to put any forward estimates in their budget. This makes the budget essentially a backward-looking average with no planning value. Prompt: "What is the most likely thing that will be different about your business in six months — something you can see evidence of today?" Almost everyone can identify one.

---

### Lesson 5.4 — Cash Flow: The Real Vital Sign

**Core concept:** Cash flow and profit are different things. A business can show a profit on paper while the bank account is depleted. The gap is almost always a timing gap — revenue earned but not yet received.

**Timing:** ~80–85 minutes. This lesson requires students to work from bank statements directly. Students who did not bring records to Lesson 5.1 and who have still not organized them will find this lesson harder. If a student is stuck on obtaining records, the Tech Concierge can help them access online banking and pull statement data.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who say "I don't have invoicing clients — I get paid at the time of service."** Cash-on-delivery businesses have smaller timing gaps, but they still have vulnerability months caused by lumpy expenses (annual insurance, quarterly taxes, large supply orders). Direct them to Exercise 2 specifically — the vulnerable month exists for almost every business, even ones with fast payment cycles.

- **Students who conflate cash flow with checking account balance.** The checking account balance includes everything — lines of credit drawn, transfers from savings, etc. Cash flow planning means tracking only what came from actual business activity. It is worth clarifying this distinction in office hours or community thread replies.

---

### Lesson 5.5 — Your Working Budget and the Strategy for Profit

**Core concept:** Profit is not optional for a sustainable business — it is the condition that allows everything meaningful about the business to continue. The working budget brings the week's work together into a forward-looking three-month plan with specific profit adjustments.

**Timing:** ~85–90 minutes. The working budget grid is the largest exercise in the module. Students who have completed Lessons 5.1–5.4 thoroughly will move through it faster; students who skimmed earlier lessons will struggle here. Recommend doing this lesson in a single uninterrupted session.

**Personal story placement:** The mini-story slot in Lesson 5.5 contains a placeholder for Chaz's personal story. This is the lesson's emotional anchor — a moment from Chaz's own experience where managing toward profitability made a practical difference. The story should be honest about what the business actually looked like before the budget discipline was in place, not just the resolution. Students at this point in the module respond to authenticity more than inspiration. See placement note below.

**Story placement note:** Replace the placeholder block in lesson_5.5.md between Exercise 2 and the Journal Prompts with a 150–250 word personal story from Chaz. Suggested theme from the source material: a moment when getting the budget right — taking profit seriously as a goal — changed a business outcome. The Warhol frame from the module introduction is already established; Chaz's story is the ground-level version of the same idea.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students with net losses in all three months.** This is distressing but not necessarily fatal. The useful reframe: "This budget is showing you the reality before you make changes. Let's look at Adjustment 1 and 2 together — which one could you implement in the next thirty days?" Sometimes one price increase or one eliminated subscription changes the margin meaningfully.

- **Students who are satisfied with breakeven.** Some students see "zero net profit" as success. For a retiree with supplemental income, that may genuinely be acceptable — but it is worth naming explicitly. If the goal is breakeven, that is a strategy choice. If the goal is income beyond cost recovery, breakeven is not enough. Let the student define what profit means for their situation, then plan for that.

## Community Thread Topics for Week 5

- Lesson 5.1: "One thing my numbers showed me that I did not expect was [observation]."
- Lesson 5.2: "The trend I am most confident about — and the one I am least sure how to plan for."
- Lesson 5.3: "My most significant probable change for the next twelve months, and why I consider it probable rather than speculative."
- Lesson 5.4: "The cash flow timing issue I discovered, and what I plan to do about it."
- Lesson 5.5: "After building the working budget, the clearest picture I now have — and the one adjustment I am committing to."

The Lesson 5.5 thread tends to be the most substantive community conversation of the module. Students who have done the work honestly often write detailed posts that help others calibrate their own numbers against comparable businesses. Encourage specificity.

## Assessment Notes

Module 5 has no graded assessment. The deliverables are:

1. The three-month Revenue and Expense worksheet (Lesson 5.1) — private, in the student's workbook.
2. The trend ranking and probable changes lists (Lessons 5.2–5.3) — private.
3. The three-month Cash Flow assessment (Lesson 5.4) — private.
4. The completed Working Budget with profit adjustments (Lesson 5.5) — private.
5. Community thread posts for each lesson — the accountability markers.

Students who have posted in the Lesson 5.5 community thread with a specific profit adjustment they are committing to have almost certainly done the underlying work. Students who post only a vague observation ("this was a lot to think about") likely have not completed the budget.

## Connection to Other Modules

Module 5 connects directly backward to Module 4 (Marketing: Five P's). The pricing discussions in Lesson 4.2 directly feed into the revenue side of the budget in Lesson 5.1. Students whose pricing was set without reference to their fixed and variable costs will discover that gap here. This is intentional — Week 5 is where the pricing work of Week 4 becomes testable.

Module 5 connects forward to Module 6 (Finding Customers). The revenue side of the working budget created in Lesson 5.5 has a built-in customer volume assumption. If that assumption is going to hold, the customer pipeline built in Module 6 needs to support it. Students who arrive at Module 6 with a specific revenue target from their working budget will engage with the lead generation work more concretely.
