# Lesson 5.1 — Revenues, Expenses, and Your First Budget Picture

### Estimated Time: Approximately 85–90 minutes

## Lesson Overview

Picture your business at its best. You operate with complete integrity. You are well-regarded in your community. Your customers are loyal and your product genuinely helps people.

Now ask: where is the money in that picture?

If your answer is vague — "coming in from customers" — you are describing most people who start a business and still, somehow, go broke. A business without a clear financial picture is not just risky; it is operating on hope rather than information.

This lesson builds the financial picture. Not a complicated one — but an honest one. You are going to list what comes in, what goes out in predictable ways, and what goes out in variable ways. Then you are going to attach real numbers to all of it.

By the end of Lesson 5.1, you will have:

- A working vocabulary for the three categories of business finance: revenues, fixed expenses, and variable expenses.
- A three-month baseline of your own numbers organized into those categories.
- An average monthly figure for each line item — the foundation your budget will rest on for the rest of this module.

## Part 1 — The Missing Picture

Here is what a surprisingly large number of small business owners have in their heads instead of a budget:

*My customers pay me. I pay my bills. What is left over is mine.*

That is not wrong. But it is incomplete in the same way that "the car goes forward when you press the pedal" is an incomplete understanding of how a car works. It gets you down the road right up until the moment it does not — and then you have no idea what broke.

A budget is not about complexity. It is about clarity. It tells you, in advance, how money moves through your business — what comes in, what goes out predictably, what goes out in response to activity level, and what the difference between those things looks like over time.

Without that picture, you can be busy, respected, and even popular, and still run out of cash. The discipline of this week is to build that picture before you need it to save you.

### Revenues

Revenue is the income your business generates. For most small businesses, this means one or two things: fees charged for services, or money received for goods sold. Both are revenues. Neither is complicated.

What matters is distinguishing revenue from money you think you might receive. Revenue is cash that has actually come in, or invoiced amounts you are confident will be paid within a defined period. Anticipated money that is not yet real is not revenue — it is optimism, and optimism does not pay fixed expenses.

When you list your revenues in today's exercise, include only what you have actually received — or, for newer businesses, your honest best estimate based on what you have seen so far. Do not inflate the number to make the budget look better. The budget's job is to show you what is real, not what you would prefer.

### Fixed Expenses

Fixed expenses are costs that stay relatively stable from month to month regardless of how busy or slow your business is. These are the obligations that show up whether you have a good month or a difficult one.

Common fixed expenses for a small business include: rent or workspace costs, phone and internet service, subscriptions and software tools, insurance, and any regular contracted services. If you have an employee or part-time assistant, their base pay is a fixed expense. If you have taken on a loan to fund the business, the debt payment is fixed.

The defining feature of a fixed expense is predictability. You know approximately what it will cost each month. That makes it the easiest category to account for — and also the most unforgiving, because it does not adjust down when business slows.

### Variable Expenses

Variable expenses move in response to your business activity. When you are busy, they go up. When things slow down, they may go down. This category includes the costs of producing and delivering what you sell — materials, supplies, per-unit costs of service delivery, and anything else that scales with volume.

For a service business, variable expenses often include things like travel costs per client, printing and materials per engagement, or subscription tools that charge per user or transaction. For a product-based business, they include the cost of materials or inventory that goes directly into what you sell.

Variable expenses require a different kind of attention than fixed ones. They are harder to predict precisely, but they tend to tell you something important: when variable expenses are rising faster than revenues, something in your model is off — either your pricing, your volume, or your delivery efficiency.

## Part 2 — Reading What the Numbers Tell You

Most people who complete Exercise 1 for the first time have one of three reactions.

The first reaction is surprise: the numbers are different — often worse — than what was assumed. This is useful. A surprise in a spreadsheet is far less damaging than a surprise in your bank account.

The second reaction is relief: the numbers are approximately what was expected, and seeing them organized and labeled makes the picture feel manageable rather than vague. This is also useful. Clarity reduces anxiety, even when the numbers need work.

The third reaction is avoidance disguised as optimism: "Those numbers don't really represent what my business will look like in a few months." This is the most dangerous reaction, because it treats the budget as something to escape rather than something to use. If your numbers are not yet where you want them, the budget does not make that worse — it makes it visible. And visible problems are solvable ones.

Before moving to Exercise 2, spend a few minutes looking at the relationship between your three rows:

- Is your revenue total covering both expense totals combined, with something left over?
- Which expense category is largest — fixed or variable?
- Are any single line items unexpectedly high? What would the picture look like if that line were reduced?

These are not questions to answer in a panic. They are questions to carry into the rest of this module, where Lessons 5.2 through 5.5 build directly on what you have assembled here.

## Mini-Story — Gerald and the Picture He Was Missing

Gerald had built a solid handyman service over his first year of retirement. He had good referrals, reasonable rates, and almost never had a slow week. But he was not keeping a budget. He knew roughly what he was charging and roughly what his supplies cost, and the checking account seemed to stay in a comfortable range.

In October, two things happened at once: a supplier raised material costs by twelve percent, and three clients who had owed him money from September finally paid in November instead. October looked terrible. Gerald assumed his business was declining.

When he sat down and actually mapped his three months of numbers — August, September, October — he discovered that October's apparent problem was entirely a timing issue, not a business problem. His revenue was consistent. His fixed expenses had not changed. His variable expenses had crept up with the supplier increase, but not dramatically.

What he had been missing was not more clients. It was the picture. Once he had the picture, October was not a crisis — it was a data point. He adjusted his materials sourcing, tightened his invoicing terms, and the next quarter looked like what he had always assumed things looked like, because now he could actually see it.

The picture does not fix the business. But it shows you what to fix — and that is most of the work.

## Transition to Lesson 5.2

You now have a baseline — three months of organized numbers that represent where your business actually is.

The next step is not to accept those numbers as permanent. It is to understand where they are going. Some of your numbers will move in predictable patterns — by season, by customer demographics, by market conditions — and those patterns already exist in your data if you know how to look for them.

Lesson 5.2 teaches you how to spot the trends that are already present in your business and use them to project with confidence rather than guess with hope.
