## Lesson Overview

There is an old Japanese proverb: "He who can see three days ahead will be rich for three thousand years."

None of us will be around to test the three-thousand-year claim. But the three-day principle is worth taking seriously.

Most small business owners either over-predict — building elaborate forecasts based on best-case assumptions — or under-predict, avoiding the discipline entirely because it feels like guessing. Neither serves the business. The discipline that actually works is narrower and more honest: look at what is already happening around you, identify the changes that are reasonably likely in the next several months, and put them into your numbers in a conservative and specific way.

That is what this lesson is about. Not clairvoyance. Not denial. Reasonable prediction based on the evidence you already have.

By the end of Lesson 5.3, you will have:

- A written list of probable changes — to your customer base, your local economy, your business structure, and your product — that are likely to affect your numbers in the next twelve months.
- A framework for distinguishing high-probability predictions from speculation.
- A set of inputs ready to incorporate into the working budget you will build in Lesson 5.5.

---

## Part 1 — The Difference Between a Prediction and a Guess

People avoid predicting because they fear being wrong. That fear usually rests on a misunderstanding of what a prediction is supposed to do.

A prediction in a budget is not a bet. It is not a statement that this specific thing will happen. It is a planning assumption: *given what I can see from here, this is a reasonable scenario to account for, and I will build my numbers accordingly.*

The prediction does not need to be right in every detail. It needs to be honest and specific enough to be useful. A prediction that says "business will probably be slower in the second quarter" is not a planning assumption — it is a feeling. A prediction that says "based on my past two years, revenue in April and May typically runs about twenty percent below the annual monthly average, so I will plan for that and hold fixed expenses steady through those months" is a planning assumption you can act on.

The test of a useful prediction is not whether it proves correct. It is whether it was specific enough to generate a concrete response in advance.

### What to predict

There are four areas where prediction is most useful for a small business budget:

**Customer base changes.** Are you gaining or losing clients? Are the clients you are most likely to gain different in size, budget, or buying behavior from your current base? If you expect to add clients, how soon and at what revenue level? If some clients are winding down or moving away, when does that revenue disappear?

**Local and regional economy.** What is happening in your community that will affect whether your potential customers have money to spend with you? A new employer moving in, a major business closing, a change in the demographic composition of the area — all of these are observable before they fully arrive.

**Business structure changes.** Are you planning to add a part-time assistant, a new service line, or a piece of equipment? Each of these has an expense side and a revenue side, and both belong in your forward budget at the point they are expected to happen.

**Product and service changes.** Are you planning to raise prices? Add a new offering? Discontinue something? Each of these is a predictable change you can build in at the appropriate month rather than waiting for it to surface in the actuals.

---

## Part 2 — Conservative and Specific

The most useful predictions are conservative on the revenue side and realistic on the expense side.

Conservative on revenues means: when you expect a positive change — a new contract, a new client segment, a price increase — build in a version of it that assumes slower uptake or smaller initial impact than your optimistic case. If you expect to sign three new clients in Q2, budget as though you will sign two. The third, if it happens, makes your numbers better than projected. If it does not happen, you are not in a hole.

Realistic on expenses means: when you expect costs to go up — a supplier increase, a new tool, a part-time assistant — do not soften that number. Put in the actual expected cost at the actual expected time. The place for optimism in a budget is not the expense line.

The other discipline worth building is distinguishing between *probable* changes and *possible* ones. Probable changes are things that are already in motion — a client who has told you they are reducing their budget, a lease renewal coming up, a trade show you have already registered for. Possible changes are things that might happen but are not yet confirmed. Probable changes go into the budget. Possible changes go on a watch list.

## Mini-Story — Victor and the New Neighborhood

Victor had spent most of his career in commercial real estate. When he retired, he started a small relocation consulting service helping people navigate the practical side of moving — vendors, neighborhood orientation, utility setup, local services. He charged a flat fee and kept the business deliberately modest: four to six clients per month, work he could do from home, no employees.

In the spring of his second year, he noticed that a large residential development was under construction four miles away — 340 homes, projected to be finished in phases over eighteen months. He had read the announcement when it came out and filed it mentally as neighborhood news. He had not thought of it as a business prediction.

Then he did the math. 340 homes, roughly 75 percent of which would involve buyers moving from out of the area. If even ten percent of those buyers would benefit from his service, that was 25 potential clients phased over 18 months — almost two additional clients per month at his current fee, without any change to his model.

He did not triple his capacity in anticipation. He made a conservative prediction: he would see two to three additional inquiry calls per month starting in month seven of construction, when closings would begin. He updated his budget to reflect modest revenue growth at that point. He also updated his expenses, anticipating that he would need to spend more time on intake and coordination.

The development absorbed into his practice without drama. He had not predicted it exactly right — closings ran about three weeks later than the public schedule suggested — but he had been positioned and prepared rather than scrambling to respond. The three days ahead had been enough.
