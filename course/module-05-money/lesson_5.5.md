## Lesson Overview

This week has been building toward one document: a working budget that reflects where your business actually is, where it is going, and what it will take to make it produce a sustainable profit.

Not a profit someday. A profit this quarter — or, if the conditions are not yet right for that, a clear picture of what needs to change before profit becomes possible.

Some people who arrive at this lesson feel an unexpected discomfort. The word "profit" sounds like it belongs to corporations or to people more aggressive than they think of themselves. But profit is not aggression. Profit is what keeps the business alive. It is what allows you to serve more clients, invest in better tools, and sustain the work beyond the point where enthusiasm alone would have run out. Without profit, even a business that genuinely helps people eventually stops.

You have spent four days this week looking honestly at your numbers. Today you put those numbers into a three-month forward budget and you decide — specifically, with real numbers — what your strategy is for making those months profitable.

By the end of Lesson 5.5, you will have:

- A completed three-month working budget that incorporates your baseline, your trends, your predictions, and your cash flow timing.
- A written profit strategy — at least two specific adjustments that improve your net position.
- A clear view of what the quarter following this one looks like if the budget holds.

## Part 1 — Why Profit Needs a Strategy

A business does not become profitable by accident. It becomes profitable because the person running it makes deliberate choices that align revenues with costs in a way that consistently leaves something over.

For a small business owned by a retiree, the profit conversation sometimes comes with a layer of ambivalence. You are not in this for the money alone. The business is meaningful. You want to help people. You value the relationships more than the income.

All of that can be true, and profit still matters. Here is the honest version of why:

A business that does not cover its costs eventually stops. When it stops, it can no longer help anyone. The clients who depended on it have to find someone else, often at more cost and inconvenience. The work you found meaningful disappears. The profit was not the point — but it was the condition that made the point possible.

Managing a business into profitability is not about greed. It is about sustainability. The question this lesson asks is a practical one: given the honest picture you have built this week, what specific choices will you make to ensure this business can continue?

### What goes into a working budget

Your working budget incorporates:

- Your average monthly revenues and expenses from Lesson 5.1 — the baseline
- Your seasonal patterns from Lesson 5.2 — layered in at the appropriate months
- Your probable changes from Lesson 5.3 — added at the specific months where they are expected
- Your cash flow timing adjustments from Lesson 5.4 — reflected in the cash position row

The budget is not a prediction that will prove exactly right. It is a planning tool. Its job is to show you what is likely, in enough detail to make decisions in advance rather than in response to surprises.

## Part 2 — The Art Behind the Art

Andy Warhol once said: "Making money is art, and good business is the best art." He said it in the context of his own transition from starving artist to commercial force — the point at which he stopped pretending that the money side of his work was less important than the creative side.

The discipline of building a budget, tracking cash flow, and projecting forward is not the glamorous part of running a business. But it is the part that makes everything else sustainable. The people who do it well — who look at their numbers honestly, plan for what they can see coming, and make adjustments before they are forced to — are not more talented or more driven than those who do not. They are simply better informed about the reality of their own situation.

Your working budget is not a bureaucratic exercise. It is the art of running a business well.
