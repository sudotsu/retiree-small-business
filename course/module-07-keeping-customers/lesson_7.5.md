# Lesson 7.5 — Staying on Your Customers' Radar

### Estimated Time: Approximately 80–85 minutes

## Lesson Overview

Consider what it takes to build a lasting personal relationship. You do not show up once, make a good impression, and then disappear for two years. You stay in contact. Not constantly — just consistently enough that the other person knows you are present and that the relationship matters to you.

A business relationship is no different. The customer who chose you once will choose you again if you remain visible to them in a positive way. The customer who once chose you but hasn't heard from you in a year is no longer your customer in any meaningful sense — they are simply someone who used your service once and may or may not remember your name.

This lesson brings together everything you have built this week into a comprehensive customer retention strategy. Not a list of good intentions — a written plan that addresses all five elements of keeping a customer: converting them from a lead, serving them well, understanding their long-term value, developing their advocacy, and staying on their radar consistently over time.

By the end of Lesson 7.5, you will have:

- A completed Customer Retention Strategy covering all five dimensions of the customer relationship.
- A clear, written answer to how you will stay visible to your customers between transactions.
- One specific action you will take this week to act on the strategy you have built.

## Part 1 — What "Staying on Their Radar" Actually Means

Staying on your customers' radar does not mean advertising at them. It means being visible to them in a way that reinforces your value, maintains the relationship, and keeps your name in their minds for the moment they — or someone they know — needs what you offer.

There is a difference between being annoying and being present. Annoying is repeated, irrelevant, or purely self-promotional contact. Present is occasional, relevant, and human contact that gives the customer something — information they can use, an acknowledgment of their history with your business, a simple gesture that says the relationship continues.

A few things that keep small businesses on their customers' radar effectively:

A seasonal check-in — a brief note or call at a time of year that is naturally significant to your customer base or your business. Not a promotion. A genuine check-in.

A follow-up after a transaction — not a survey, not a form, but a personal contact to see how things went. This is almost universally underused by small businesses and almost universally appreciated by customers who receive it.

Useful information — something related to your customers' interests that arrives from you but is not advertising your business directly. A useful article, a community event they might care about, a note about something you thought they would appreciate. The act of thinking of them is itself the communication.

An acknowledgment of milestones — a client's anniversary with your business, a birthday if you know it, a note of congratulations on something significant in their life. These small gestures have an outsized effect on how remembered and valued customers feel.

What matters is not the frequency or the budget. It is the consistency and the genuine intention behind the contact. Customers who feel seen and valued are customers who stay.

## Mini-Story — Helen and the Customers She Almost Lost

Helen had operated a small travel consulting practice for eleven years, helping clients — primarily retirees and pre-retirees — plan international and domestic trips. She was excellent at finding customers: a small ad in the community newsletter, a table at the library's travel lecture series twice a year, and a steady stream of word of mouth from happy clients.

She had no strategy for keeping them.

The relationship after a trip was complete was essentially silence. No follow-up. No seasonal check-in. No newsletter. No acknowledgment that a client who had traveled with her twice was worth treating differently than a first-time caller. She thought of her job as ending when the client boarded the plane.

In her ninth year, three long-standing clients mentioned in the same month that they had booked trips with an online service. Not because she had failed them — they were happy with her work. Because they had not heard from her, and the online service had sent them a note right when they were thinking about travel.

Helen spent a weekend building a simple system: a seasonal postcard twice a year, a brief personal follow-up call two weeks after each trip, and a birthday note to any client whose birthday she had on file. None of it required more than a few hours a month.

In the two years that followed, she had her best retention numbers since opening. She had not improved the trips. She had simply refused to disappear between them.
