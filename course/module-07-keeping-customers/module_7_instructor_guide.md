# Module 7 Instructor Guide — Keeping Customers

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 7**

*For Chaz. Not for students.*

---

## Module Overview

Module 7 is the customer retention week. Students work through the full arc of a customer relationship: converting leads, delivering service that makes customers stay, understanding long-term customer value, developing evangelists, and maintaining visibility between transactions. The module produces four working documents: a pitch, a customer service strategy, a lifetime value calculation, and a comprehensive retention strategy.

The through-line is that customer retention is not passive — it is a system of deliberate practices. The module pushes students from vague good intentions ("I'll stay in touch") toward specific, named, actionable commitments.

## Lesson-by-Lesson Facilitation Notes

### Lesson 7.1 — Turning Leads into Customers

**What the lesson does:** Introduces the six elements that convert a lead into a customer. Students complete a pitch development worksheet and then write their actual pitch as a narrative.

**Common student patterns:**
- Most students have not thought about the pitch as a structured process with specific elements. They have relied on being knowledgeable and friendly and hoping the lead comes to a decision on their own. The lesson surfaces how much of that conversion process is left unmanaged.
- The biggest gap in most students' pitches will be the offer — the clear, specific invitation to take the next step. Probe this in discussion: "What specifically does someone do to become your customer?"
- Students who have been in business for years sometimes resist this exercise because they feel their approach already works. Ask them: what percentage of your interested leads actually become customers? That number is usually lower than they expect.

**Opening Module 7:** Good opening question from the previous week's commitment: "Did you take the first-thirty-days action you committed to in Module 6? What happened?" This creates accountability and often produces instructive stories — both successes and honest misses.

**Community thread:** Students share the weakest element in their pitch and their specific offer. The specific-offer answers are often revealing.

### Lesson 7.2 — Customer Service That Keeps Customers

**What the lesson does:** Builds a customer service strategy across five components — people, phone, web, communications, and other. Uses the Jeff story from the source to illustrate how personal service creates customer loyalty.

**Common student patterns:**
- Most students have a reasonable answer for "people" and "phone" but weak or absent answers for "communications" — the ongoing outreach between transactions. This is where most retention leaks occur.
- The "if I were my own customer" journal prompt often produces the most honest self-assessment students make in the entire module. Create space for this in discussion.
- Students who do not yet have a web presence may feel this section does not apply to them. Redirect: the question is what a prospective customer experiences when they look for your business online. Even "nothing comes up" is an answer that has implications.

**Community thread:** Students share the weakest component and the honest "what I'd want as my own customer" answer.

---

### Lesson 7.3 — The Lifetime Value of a Customer

**What the lesson does:** Introduces the lifetime value calculation and the three practices that extend customer relationships. Students do the math for their own business and write a draft letter to their best customers.

**Common student patterns:**
- The lifetime value calculation is often the lesson's most impactful moment. Students who have never done this arithmetic frequently express surprise at the number. Allow time for this in discussion.
- The letter exercise is sometimes treated as an academic exercise rather than something students consider actually sending. The lesson encourages consideration of sending it — push gently on this. The letters that students actually send typically produce visible results.
- Students with very new businesses may feel they cannot calculate lifetime value meaningfully. Encourage realistic estimation with explicit uncertainty: "Based on what you know about your business model, if a customer stayed five years, what would they be worth?"

**Community thread:** Students share their lifetime value calculation and the action the math is prompting them to take.

---

### Lesson 7.4 — Turning Your Best Customers into Evangelists

**What the lesson does:** Introduces the three elements that develop an evangelist — information, privileged relationship, recognition — and asks students to write a specific plan for five real customers.

**Common student patterns:**
- The exercise requires students to name real customers in writing, which surfaces commitment. Students who write generic descriptions ("my most loyal client") rather than names often have not fully engaged with the exercise.
- The recognition-not-payment distinction sometimes sparks discussion. Some students will have customers who expect payment for referrals. The lesson's answer: those customers have a different relationship with the business than natural evangelists do, and should be treated accordingly — but the majority of enthusiastic customers respond well to acknowledgment rather than payment.
- Glenn's story often resonates strongly with students who recognize the pattern: they have enthusiastic clients who are already trying to advocate for them, but have never received the deliberate attention that would activate them fully.

**Community thread:** Students describe a potential evangelist (without naming them) and the first specific action they'll take.

---

### Lesson 7.5 — Staying on Your Customers' Radar

**What the lesson does:** Synthesis lesson. Students complete a five-part written retention strategy that consolidates all of the module's work, and commit to a specific action this week.

**Common student patterns:**
- The retention strategy often reveals that students have strong plans for some elements (usually pitch and customer service) and vague plans for others (usually the staying-visible element). This is normal — the goal is to make the gap visible and write a specific answer for it.
- The "this week's action" commitment should be genuinely specific. "I'll work on my customer relationships more" is not a commitment. "I'll call [Customer Name] by [date] to personally thank them for their referral last month" is a commitment.
- The Modules 6 and 7 connection is worth naming explicitly in any live discussion: Modules 6 and 7 together describe a complete customer cycle. The question of which half of that cycle students need to strengthen more is a useful reflection prompt.

**Community thread:** Students share their this-week action and the retention element they have been most neglecting.

## Module Connections

**Module 6 connection:** Students reference their customer profile (Lesson 6.2) and profiled message (Lesson 6.3) in building their pitch in Lesson 7.1. If any student did not complete Module 6, direct them back to those lessons before starting Module 7.

**Module 5 connection:** The customer retention strategy from Lesson 7.5 has budget implications. The cost of customer outreach activities — seasonal cards, thank-you lunches for evangelists, follow-up calls — belongs in the operating expense section of the Module 5 budget. Students who completed Module 5 thoroughly may want to revisit their budget to account for these items.

**Module 8 preview:** Module 8 asks students to bring together everything from the course into a working action plan. The retention strategy from Module 7 is one of the inputs. Students should keep their written retention strategy accessible for Module 8.

---

## Assessment Notes

Module 7 produces five written outputs: pitch, customer service strategy, lifetime value calculation, evangelist development plan, and retention strategy. The quality of those documents is the primary evidence of engagement. Generic answers ("I'll serve customers well") are not acceptable substitutes for specific ones ("I'll make a personal follow-up call within two weeks of every completed project").

The this-week action in Lesson 7.5 is a useful Module 8 opener: "Did you do it?"
