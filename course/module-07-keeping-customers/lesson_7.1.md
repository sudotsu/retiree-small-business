# Lesson 7.1 — Turning Leads into Customers

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 25 minutes | Exercises: 35 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

A lead is not a customer. A lead is a person who is aware that your business exists and has some degree of interest in what you offer. That is a starting point, not a result.

The gap between a lead who knows about you and a customer who pays you is crossed through a deliberate process. Not a trick. Not a script. A structured approach that gives the lead every reasonable opportunity to say yes — by engaging them in familiar territory, speaking to their actual needs, addressing the concerns they have before they express them, and finishing with a clear offer.

Most businesses lose leads not because the product is wrong but because the process from first contact to commitment is vague. The lead is interested, then nothing clear happens, and the interest evaporates.

By the end of Lesson 7.1, you will have:

- A working understanding of the six elements that move a lead toward becoming a customer.
- Completed answers to the six questions that define your pitch for your specific business.
- A written draft of your pitch — the narrative you use to move someone from awareness to commitment.

---

## Part 1 — The Six Elements of a Converting Pitch

Each of these six elements addresses one thing that leads need before they say yes. Leave any of them out, and the process develops a leak.

**1. Engage leads in their comfort zones.** Where does the conversation happen — in person, by phone, by email, in a familiar community setting? What language do they speak — formal, casual, technical, plain? The lead is more likely to say yes when the conversation is happening in a place and a register they recognize. This is not about being someone you are not. It is about choosing the setting and language that puts the other person at ease.

**2. Talk to the head and the heart.** A lead needs two things to commit: objective information and an emotional reason. The objective information is the factual case — what the product does, what it costs, why it is reliable. The emotional reason is the feeling — the satisfaction of solving a problem, the relief of having something handled, the confidence that comes from the right choice. Most business pitches are heavy on facts and thin on emotional connection, or the reverse. Both are necessary.

**3. Address customer needs as specifically as possible.** A pitch that describes your business in general terms leaves the lead to figure out on their own whether any of it applies to them. A pitch that describes their specific situation — and then shows how your business addresses it — does the work for them. You have built a customer profile. You know the situation your typical customer is in. Use that knowledge to make the lead feel understood.

**4. Show how your product or service meets the customer's needs in terms of benefits, not features.** A feature is what the product has or does. A benefit is what that feature means to the customer. "We are open on weekends" is a feature. "You can reach us when it is convenient for you, without rearranging your week" is the benefit. Leads respond to benefits — the improvement to their situation — far more than to features, which require them to translate the relevance themselves.

**5. Remove objections to completing the sale.** Every lead has at least one reason they might not commit, and most will not voice it directly. Price, timing, uncertainty about whether the product is really the right fit, a prior bad experience with a similar service — these are common objections. The pitch does not need to address every possible objection, but it should address the most probable one. Your customer profile tells you what that objection is likely to be.

**6. Make the offer.** This is the element most often skipped, and the one most directly responsible for leads who stay interested indefinitely without ever committing. An offer is a specific, clear statement of what the lead needs to do to move forward: "Here is how you get started," "Call this number by Friday and we will schedule the first appointment," "This is the next step." Without a specific offer, the lead has to manufacture their own next step — and most do not.

## Mini-Story — Marcus and the Leads That Never Closed

Marcus had been doing landscape design for twenty-two years when he retired from his corporate job and started taking on private residential clients. He was good — genuinely talented — and the leads came in consistently. He presented at two HOA community nights each spring, got regular calls from people who had heard his name, and collected business cards at the local garden show.

He closed almost none of them.

The presentations went well. People were interested. They asked questions. They took his card. Then weeks passed and nothing came of it. Marcus would follow up once, get a polite reply, and the exchange would fade.

When a friend who ran a small consulting business asked Marcus to walk through what happened after the presentation, the problem became obvious: Marcus gave a compelling overview of his work, answered every question well, and then — nothing. No description of what the next step looked like. No offer. No specific invitation to begin. The lead was interested and waiting for a door to open, and Marcus had never built a door.

He added one paragraph to every follow-up — a clear, specific description of what the first meeting would look like, what it would cost, and how to schedule it. Three of his next five follow-ups became paying clients.

The leads had been there all along. The pitch had a missing step.
