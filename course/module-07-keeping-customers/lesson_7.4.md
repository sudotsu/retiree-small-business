# Lesson 7.4 — Turning Your Best Customers into Evangelists

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 25 minutes | Exercises: 35 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

In every customer base, there are a few customers who are not just satisfied — they are genuinely enthusiastic. They know your business well. They have strong opinions about what makes it good. They mention you to people without being asked, and when they do, the recommendation carries weight because it is obviously genuine.

These customers are close to becoming evangelists for your business. They need two things to complete that transition: permission and a few tools.

This lesson is about how to give them both. Not through payment or formal incentive programs, but through the three things that most consistently turn a dedicated customer into someone who actively promotes your business — information, a sense of privileged connection, and recognition for the referrals they generate.

By the end of Lesson 7.4, you will have:

- A clear understanding of what distinguishes an evangelist from a satisfied customer.
- A written plan for five of your best customers, outlining the specific actions you will take with each.
- A realistic sense of what it would take to begin activating your potential evangelists this week.

---

## Part 1 — What Makes an Evangelist

An evangelist is a customer who has become a voluntary promoter of your business. They do not require payment to mention you. They are not part of a formal referral program. They recommend you because they genuinely value what you provide and have enough confidence in their knowledge of your business to stake their own reputation on the recommendation.

Think about the customers you know who fit this description in other businesses — the person who tells everyone about a specific plumber, or a particular accountant, or a restaurant they have been going to for fifteen years. They do not wait to be asked. They bring it up. And when they recommend, the people they are talking to listen, because the recommendation is coming from someone they trust who has real experience.

What turns a satisfied customer into an evangelist is usually not one dramatic gesture. It is the accumulated feeling of being genuinely valued, well-informed, and personally connected to the business. Three things accelerate that accumulation:

**1. Detailed information about your product or service.** Enthusiastic customers want to know more than the average customer. They want to understand what you do, why it works, what makes your approach different. Giving them that information — a behind-the-scenes conversation, a detailed explanation of your process, a look at something others do not usually see — deepens their investment in your business and gives them the substance to be genuinely persuasive when they recommend you.

**2. Evidence of a privileged relationship.** This is not about special discounts or exclusive pricing. It is about the signal that this customer is genuinely known to you — that you remember their name, their preferences, their history with your business. A phone call from the owner. An advance notice about something new. Being treated as a person with a history in your business rather than as an anonymous transaction. The feeling of being on the inside of something.

**3. Recognition for their referrals and recommendations.** When a dedicated customer refers someone to your business and that referral becomes a customer, the referring customer should hear about it. Not a financial payment, in most cases — what customers who want to be recognized want is acknowledgment. A thank-you call. A note. Being taken to coffee or lunch once or twice a year. The small, personal gestures that say: your advocacy for this business is noticed and valued.

This is one of the most neglected areas in small business customer development. The assumption is that only large companies can treat customers this way. The reverse is often true: the size of a large company is what makes personal recognition at this level nearly impossible for them to deliver. A small business owner has a structural advantage — the ability to know their customers by name and act accordingly.

## Mini-Story — Glenn and One Focused Morning

Glenn had been running a small dog training operation for four years, working with residential clients — mostly families and recently retired dog owners — out of a rented training space twice a week. He had steady business, genuinely good results, and a growing reputation in his area through word of mouth he had never deliberately cultivated.

He could name, without thinking hard, the three clients who had been most openly enthusiastic about his work. One woman had mentioned him to her entire neighborhood association. An older gentleman had brought two separate family members to Glenn's beginner class over two years. A third had posted about him on the local community board twice without being asked.

Glenn had thanked them, of course. But he had never done anything specific in response to their advocacy. He had never called to say their referrals had become clients. He had never shared anything with them that his average client did not also receive. They knew him the way any client knows a service provider — not the way an evangelist knows a business they are invested in.

Glenn spent one morning making three phone calls. He told each of them about something behind the work — a training philosophy he had developed, a technique he had refined after years of practice, why he approached dogs the way he did. He mentioned, specifically, that he knew they had referred people to him and that he wanted them to know how much that meant to the business. He invited each of them to bring a dog to an upcoming advanced session as his guests.

In the following month, Glenn received more referrals than in the previous six. He had not changed his methods, his prices, or his schedule. He had given three people who wanted to be advocates the information and recognition that activated the role they had already been trying to play.
