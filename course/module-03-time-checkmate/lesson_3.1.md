## Lesson Overview

In Module 1, you mapped the internal patterns — the defaults, beliefs, and decision drivers — that have been running your life. In Module 2, you built a picture of what your life and your business actually look like, and you wrote five objectives to move from where you are toward where you want to be.

This week is about time.

Not time management in the abstract sense — the kind of thing addressed in every business seminar you have ever attended, with color-coded calendars and prioritization matrices. This is something more specific: an honest look at where your time is actually going right now, so you can make deliberate decisions about where it should go instead.

You cannot build a business — or protect a life — on a schedule you have never written down.

This week you will:
- Write out your current schedule honestly, including everything that actually happens in a typical week.
- Identify which of your default settings and big picture goals deserve protected time.
- Clear out the habits and patterns that consume time without producing value.
- Make explicit commitments to both your business and your personal life.
- Rewrite a working schedule that serves your life instead of running over it.

Lesson 3.1 begins the process. Today you write out where your time is actually going.

By the end of this lesson, you will have:
- Completed an honest audit of your current weekly schedule.
- Identified where your time is being spent and whether the distribution reflects your priorities.
- Noted what surprises you — the categories where you spend more or less time than you expected.

## Part 1 — The Problem With "More Time"

Everyone wants more time. Writers facing deadlines beg their editors for it. Parents who mean to be more present look for it. People with a business idea sitting in a notebook promise themselves they will get to it when they find it.

But more time is not the problem, and finding it is not the solution.

The problem is that most people have never stopped to look honestly at where their time is actually going. They operate from a general sense of how their week is structured — a rough mental sketch — and that sketch is almost always incomplete and often significantly wrong.

Think about the last time you sat down with your calendar and added up, hour by hour, how your typical week was actually spent. Not how you planned it. Not how you intended it. How it actually went.

Most people have not done this since the last time a seminar or a book asked them to. And the seminar before that asked them to do the same thing. And they may have filled it in dutifully and then watched the schedule they made drift away within a week or two, un-implemented.

This exercise is different from those for one specific reason: you are not filling in a schedule in order to optimize efficiency. You are filling it in because the work you have done in the last two weeks — your defaults, your big picture, your five objectives — has to be connected to your actual time. If the things you identified as most important in Modules 1 and 2 are not in your schedule, they will not happen. It is that simple. Not because you lack discipline or intention, but because life fills available time the way water fills a container. Whatever space is not deliberately claimed will be claimed by something else.

The first step to claiming that space is seeing clearly what is in it already.

## Part 2 — What to Include in Your Current Schedule

Be complete and be honest. This week's schedule should reflect what your typical week actually looks like in this season of your life — not what you wish it looked like, and not what it looked like five years ago.

Include all of the following that apply:

**Sleep.** This is part of your schedule. Write in your typical sleep hours. If they vary significantly by day, approximate.

**Meals.** Breakfast, lunch, dinner, and anything in between. Include time for preparation if you cook regularly.

**Health and physical activity.** Any exercise, medical appointments, physical therapy, or movement that is a regular part of your week.

**Family time.** Time with a spouse or partner, visits with adult children or grandchildren, caregiving responsibilities, and any family commitments that recur weekly.

**Community and spiritual life.** Church, civic groups, volunteer work, clubs, classes, or any regular community commitment.

**Hobbies and personal interests.** The things you do for yourself — reading, gardening, woodworking, watching sports, a creative practice.

**Obligations that are not really optional.** Errands, household maintenance, appointments, and recurring tasks that simply have to happen.

**Time that goes to habit rather than intention.** This is the category people most often leave off. If you spend two hours a day watching television, that goes in the schedule. If you spend an hour most mornings reading the newspaper or scrolling your phone, that goes in. If you have a pattern of long phone calls with a sibling or an old friend several times a week, write it down.

**Any current work.** Part-time work, consulting, freelance, or any income-producing activity you are currently doing.

**Your business time (if any).** If you are already spending time on your business — even informal thinking, research, or planning — write it in.

## Part 3 — Reading Your Schedule Honestly

Before you write in your journal today, spend a few minutes looking at what you have produced. Ask yourself these questions without rushing:

**Which category takes more of your time than you expected?** Almost everyone finds at least one surprise here. Common ones include habitual activities that take more time than assumed, caregiving responsibilities that have quietly grown, or social commitments that have become longer than they need to be.

**Which category takes less of your time than you intended?** The categories people most often find starved for time include personal health, time alone to think, and any activity connected to building or maintaining something new — including a business.

**Is there anything missing from the schedule entirely?** Something you know should be in your week that simply is not present right now? Note it specifically.

**Does this schedule match the big picture you described in Module 2?** Look at your one-sentence big picture statement from Lesson 2.2. Does the schedule you have just written reflect the life that sentence describes, or does it describe a different life? The distance between those two pictures is exactly what this week will address.

## Mini-Story — Barbara and the Hours That Disappeared

Barbara had been telling herself for eighteen months that she would start her freelance bookkeeping practice "as soon as she had more time." She was retired from twenty-two years in accounting, she had the skills, she had contacts who had already asked about her services, and she had a plan sitting in a folder on her desk.

But she did not have time — or so she believed.

When a friend suggested she actually map out her week on paper, Barbara was resistant. She was in her late sixties, not a twenty-year-old in a productivity seminar. She knew how she spent her time.

She did it anyway.

What she found surprised her. She was spending approximately twelve hours per week watching cable news — not because she was deeply invested in any of it, but because it was on. She was spending another six hours per week on phone calls with two people who regularly called her and whom she found draining but did not know how to decline. And she was spending nearly three hours each week doing tasks for an adult son who had the skills to handle them himself but knew his mother would take them on if he asked.

That was twenty-one hours per week — more than half a full-time job — going to things she had never consciously decided to prioritize.

Barbara did not need more time. She needed to see where her time was going.

She launched her practice four months later, working twelve hours a week, entirely within hours she had already had.

## Journal Prompts

Take 15 minutes. Write complete sentences for each prompt.

1. When you compare the hours you actually spend in each category against the hours you would choose to spend, what is the most significant discrepancy? Why do you think it exists?

2. Is there a category in your schedule that takes time not because you value it, but because it simply fills available space — something that happens to you more than something you choose? Describe it specifically.

3. Look at your schedule and find where your business time appears. Is it there at all? If not, where in the week might it realistically go? Be specific about which days and time blocks.

4. If the person whose schedule this is had five years of this same schedule ahead of them, what would their life look like? Is that a life you want?
