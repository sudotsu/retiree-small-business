# Lesson 3.4 — Commit to Time for Business and Yourself

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75 minutes

## Lesson Overview

The work of the first three days of this week has been diagnostic. You mapped your current schedule, identified what it must protect, and named what it can release. That work is complete.

Today the work becomes commitments.

Not intentions. Not plans. Commitments — specific time blocks, written down, assigned to specific categories of your life, that you will act on whether or not the week cooperates.

There are two categories of commitment this lesson asks for, and both are non-negotiable. The first is time for your business. The second is time for yourself and your personal life. Many people taking this course assume the business time is the harder commitment to make. For most of them, it turns out to be the other way around.

By the end of Lesson 3.4, you will have:
- Identified and written down every specific time commitment your business requires in a typical week.
- Identified and written down every specific time commitment your personal life requires — including those you have been deferring.
- Understood why both categories are equally essential and why neither can be allowed to routinely crowd out the other.

## Part 1 — Scheduling the Business

If you are going to build a business, the activities that build it must be in your schedule. Not in a general sense — "I will work on my business several times a week" — but in a specific, committed sense: this activity, this day, this time block.

The reason for this specificity is not rigidity. It is protection. A business activity that is not on the schedule competes with everything else in your week on equal footing. And everything else in your week has the advantage of already being there. The new activity — the one that has not yet produced results and that the people around you do not yet understand — will lose that competition every time.

Mark Twain put it plainly: do the thing you fear the most, and the death of fear is certain. The activities most critical to your business in its early stages are often the ones that feel most awkward, uncertain, or exposed — reaching out to potential clients, talking about your business in public, asking for referrals, pricing your services. These activities will be deferred as long as they are not protected by a specific time commitment.

Your business commitments this week should cover the full range of activities that a typical business week requires:

**Client or customer work.** The actual service or product delivery — whatever your business does for the people it serves.

**Business development.** The outreach, relationship-building, and conversation that brings new clients or customers into your world. For most early-stage businesses, this needs more protected time than people initially allocate.

**Administration.** Invoicing, record-keeping, scheduling, correspondence, and the behind-the-scenes work that keeps the business organized.

**Learning and development.** Any skill-building, research, or professional development that your business requires. This is often the first category deferred when weeks get busy — protect it explicitly.

**Planning and review.** A weekly block to look back at what happened and forward at what comes next. Brief but essential. Without it, weeks blur into months without meaningful reflection.

## Part 2 — Scheduling Yourself

This is the commitment most business owners defer, and the one they most consistently regret deferring.

There is a version of the new entrepreneur that earns the business time by sacrificing personal time. The logic is understandable: this is important, the business needs attention, there are only so many hours, something has to give. And personal time is the category that seems most flexible — it does not have a client waiting, it does not have a deadline, it can always happen next week.

The cost of this trade does not appear immediately. But it accumulates. The physical health deferred becomes the health problem that arrives later. The relationship deferred becomes the distance that is difficult to close. The rest deferred becomes the exhaustion that slows everything down. The recreation deferred becomes the sense of purposelessness that makes the business feel like the old job in new clothes.

Your personal time commitments must be scheduled the same way your business time is scheduled: specifically, in writing, with the same level of protection.

At minimum, your schedule should explicitly protect:

**Physical health.** Exercise, medical appointments, and anything your body requires to function well. For people in the 60–75 age range, this is not a lifestyle preference — it is a business infrastructure decision. If your health declines because you stopped protecting time for it, the business suffers directly.

**Key relationships.** Specific time with the people you identified as most important in your big picture. Not time that will happen when everything else is done — scheduled time, the same way a client appointment is scheduled.

**Rest and recovery.** A daily break that is not a transition between work tasks. A weekly time block that is genuinely free from obligation. This may feel like a luxury; it is not. It is maintenance.

**The activities that make you yourself.** Whatever it is that, when it is regularly absent from your week, makes you feel less like the person you intended to be. A walk. A hobby. A practice. Time in a garden, at a workbench, at a piano, on a fishing boat. This category matters, and it belongs in your schedule explicitly.

## Part 3 — Both Commitments Are Non-Negotiable

There will be weeks when something in the business demands more time than the schedule allows. There will be weeks when something in your personal life requires the same. Both of those are real, and neither can be entirely avoided.

The question is not whether exceptions will happen. They will. The question is what your default position is — what happens in a normal week, absent a genuine emergency.

If your default is to trade personal time for business time whenever the week gets busy, the personal time will consistently disappear. If your default is to protect personal time so firmly that business time becomes the first thing released when the schedule is under pressure, the business will not grow.

The practical answer is a commitment to both — held seriously, protected by default, and adjusted only for genuine exceptions rather than ordinary pressure.

What constitutes a genuine exception? Something specific and time-limited: a client emergency that genuinely could not be planned for, a health situation that requires immediate attention, a family event that legitimately cannot be rescheduled. Ordinary busyness, mild discomfort, and the general sense that there is "too much to do" do not qualify. Every week has too much to do. The schedule is what decides what actually gets done.

When an exception arises and you use your business time for personal needs, or your personal time for business needs, note it — and make a plan to recover the lost time in the same week or the following week. Do not simply accept the loss and move on. That pattern, repeated, is how commitments dissolve.

## Mini-Story — Gerald's Friday Afternoon

Gerald was sixty-six and building a small handyman service. He was organized, reliable, and quickly had more work than he had expected. Within three months of starting, he was booked solid four days a week.

He had scheduled Friday afternoons as protected personal time — a habit he had maintained through most of his working life, a long lunch and a walk with his wife that had been the ritual that marked the end of the work week for thirty years of their marriage.

In the fourth month, a good client asked if Gerald could fit in a job on a Friday afternoon. The client was pleasant, the job was quick, and the money was real.

Gerald said yes.

The following month, the same thing happened. And the month after that. Within six months, Friday afternoons had become regular work time by default — not by decision, but by a series of individual yeses that had collectively unmade a commitment Gerald had never consciously chosen to end.

His wife did not complain. But Gerald noticed the absence of the ritual in a way that was difficult to describe. Something that had been part of his sense of a well-ordered life had quietly disappeared.

He rebuilt it. He set a policy — not a rigid rule, but a genuine default: Friday afternoons are not available for client work. When asked, he said simply that he was not available on Friday afternoons. No clients pushed back.

The Friday afternoon walk was not glamorous. It was not strategic. It did not advance any objective.

It was the thing that, when it was missing, made him less good at everything else.

## Journal Prompts

Take 15 minutes. Write complete sentences.

1. Look at your two commitment tables. Is the business time total and the personal time total both realistic given your schedule? If one of them had to be reduced, which would you reduce first — and what does that tell you about where your default priorities actually are?

2. Think about a time in your life when you consistently deferred personal time in favor of work or obligation. What was the actual cost of that pattern over time?

3. If your business grows significantly in the next year, what is the personal commitment you are most at risk of losing? What would it take to protect it anyway?

4. Mark Twain's line — "do the thing you fear the most, and the death of fear is certain" — applies to business activities that feel uncomfortable. What is the specific business activity in your schedule that feels most like that? What would it mean to protect time for it anyway?
