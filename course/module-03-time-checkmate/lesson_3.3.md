# Lesson 3.3 — Clearing Space: Eliminating Time-Wasters

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 35 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

In Lesson 3.1 you saw where your time is actually going. In Lesson 3.2 you identified the six anchors — three default settings and three big picture goals — that your schedule must protect.

Today you find the space to protect them.

The space is already there. It exists in every schedule, in every week, occupied by habits and patterns that have accumulated without being examined. The goal of this lesson is not to make you more productive or to fill every hour with useful activity. The goal is specific: to recover enough time that the six anchors you identified yesterday have room to breathe, and the business you are building has room to grow.

This will be the most uncomfortable exercise of the week for many people. Naming what wastes your time requires a kind of honesty that most schedules never ask for. It is much easier to accept a schedule as given — as the natural product of your obligations — than to acknowledge that a meaningful portion of it is the product of inertia, avoidance, or habit that has simply never been questioned.

By the end of Lesson 3.3, you will have:
- Named at least seven specific habits, patterns, or obligations that consume time in your current schedule without earning their place.
- Decided which of those you want to keep, which to limit, and which to eliminate.
- Estimated how much time you will recover through these decisions.

## Part 1 — The Tyranny of Habit Over Purpose

Here is a simple question: what do you really have to do today?

Not what is on your list, not what you assumed would happen, not what the day has demanded in the past. What do you genuinely have to do today — meaning: if you did not do it, something real and important would be worse as a result?

When people work through that question honestly, the list is almost always shorter than their day. Sometimes much shorter.

The reason is that most people spend significant portions of their time doing things out of habit rather than purpose. Not laziness — habit. The two are not the same. Lazy people avoid tasks they know they should do. Habitual people do tasks they have never questioned, because those tasks have always been part of the rhythm of their days, regardless of whether that rhythm still makes sense.

Think about how telecommuting became a possibility in modern work. It required someone to ask — genuinely, not rhetorically — whether the daily commute to an office was necessary for the work to be done well. That question had not been asked, not because the answer was obviously "yes," but because commuting had always been part of the rhythm. It was invisible as a choice.

Your schedule contains similar invisibilities.

The person you call back immediately, every time, regardless of urgency — is that habit or is that a deliberate choice? The errand you run on Tuesday morning because Tuesday has always been errand day — is there a particular reason it has to be Tuesday morning? The two-hour window of television in the afternoon that started as occasional relief and became daily routine — when did you decide that was how you wanted to spend that time?

Some of these patterns are worth keeping. That is an honest possibility. The television might be the single way you genuinely decompress. The Tuesday errand might be followed by something that matters to you. The habitual phone call might be a relationship you actually value and want to protect.

But "worth keeping" should be a conscious decision — not a default outcome of never having asked the question.

The exercise today asks you to ask the question, systematically and honestly, for everything in your schedule that might be occupying time by habit rather than by deliberate choice.

## Part 2 — Three Categories of Time-Wasters

Not all time-wasters are the same, and the appropriate response to each is different.

**Category 1: Pure habits with no real value**

These are things you do primarily because you have always done them or because they require the least resistance in the moment. They do not produce anything you genuinely value; they simply fill time. The first step with these is recognition — many people discover they have been doing something for years without once consciously deciding to. The appropriate response is usually elimination or significant reduction.

Examples: Scrolling through social media out of boredom rather than connection, watching programming you would not miss if it were gone, attending meetings or social obligations you have never found worthwhile and would not choose to attend if given an honest choice.

**Category 2: Real relationships or obligations managed badly**

These are things you value — or would value — that have grown to consume more time than they need to or more time than they deserve relative to other priorities. The appropriate response here is not elimination but restructuring. The relationship is worth protecting; the current time arrangement may not be.

Examples: Phone calls with a friend or family member that regularly run ninety minutes when thirty would be satisfying; social commitments that have expanded over the years into something larger and more time-consuming than the original purpose; obligations you took on during an earlier season of life that no longer fit the current one.

**Category 3: Obligations that belong to someone else**

These are tasks, responsibilities, or time commitments that exist not because you genuinely need to do them, but because you are the person who will do them if no one else does. Someone — often an adult child, a neighbor, a volunteer committee, or an organization — has discovered that you will take on what they do not claim. The appropriate response is a clear and respectful boundary.

Examples: Routine tasks for an adult child who is capable of handling them; committee work at a club or organization that consumes significant time without producing meaningful satisfaction; being the default caregiver or organizer for extended family functions that others could share more equitably.

## Part 3 — On the Difficulty of Letting Things Go

For most people, at least one item on their list will be genuinely difficult to act on, even after they have decided it should be limited or removed.

This is worth acknowledging directly.

Sometimes the difficulty is practical — a real relationship that needs a real conversation, not just a private decision. Sometimes it is emotional — a habit that has become a form of comfort, and giving it up feels like a loss even when the loss is minor in objective terms. Sometimes it is social — an obligation that involves other people who have expectations, and meeting those expectations feels easier than changing them.

None of these difficulties make the decision wrong. They simply mean that acting on it requires something beyond writing it in a workbook. For items in this category, the useful next step is not to delay the decision but to identify specifically what the follow-through requires. A conversation with someone. A response to an email. A quiet change in routine that does not need to be announced. Whatever it is, name it and plan for it.

The cost of not acting on what you have identified is time — the same time your six anchors need, and that your business will need. Every hour you recover from something that was not earning its place is an hour you can give to something that will.

## Mini-Story — Louise and the Tuesday Obligation

Louise was sixty-three and had been the unofficial family organizer for so long that she could not remember when it had started. Every Tuesday morning, she drove forty minutes to her sister's house to help with weekly errands — grocery shopping, a pharmacy run, and usually whatever had come up since the previous Tuesday.

Her sister was healthy, drove, and had two adult children who lived nearby.

When Louise mapped her current schedule in Lesson 3.1, the Tuesday routine appeared immediately: a four-hour block, every week, for the past several years. She had not noticed it as a choice. It was simply what happened on Tuesdays.

In today's exercise she listed it under Category 3 — an obligation that belonged to someone else, that she had taken on not because it was needed but because she was the person who would do it if no one else did.

She did not remove it. She limited it: one Tuesday per month, and only for anything genuinely beyond her sister's capacity to manage independently. She had a brief, honest conversation with her sister. Her sister, to Louise's slight surprise, said she had wondered for some time if the visits were more for Louise's sense of being needed than for any real practical requirement.

Louise recovered nearly three hours per week. She used two of them for her business and one for the morning walk she had been putting off for two years.

The hardest part was the conversation. Once it happened, everything else was simple.
