## Lesson Overview

Everything you have done this week has been preparation for today.

You have an honest picture of your current schedule. You have six anchors — three default settings and three big picture goals — that your schedule must protect. You have named what can be released, and estimated the time it will free. You have made explicit commitments to both your business and your personal life.

Today you write the revised schedule.

Not a to-do list. Not a minute-by-minute plan. A working template — a general structure for your week that reflects your priorities, accommodates your commitments, and gives your calendar something meaningful to grow from.

By the end of Lesson 3.5, you will have:
- A written revised weekly schedule built around your six anchors and your Lesson 3.4 commitments.
- A clear understanding of how to use this schedule as a living document rather than a fixed rule.
- A plan for where to keep the schedule visible and how to review it as the coming weeks unfold.

## Part 1 — What This Schedule Is and Is Not

Before you build your revised schedule, it is worth being precise about what you are creating.

This is not a day-planner. You are not writing in specific client appointments, exact meeting times, or the name of the person you will call on Tuesday at 2:15. That level of detail belongs in a portable calendar — physical or digital — that you use day to day.

What you are building is a master template. It defines the structure of a typical week — the categories of activity that appear in each day and the approximate time each category receives. It is the framework your day-planner is built inside of, not the day-planner itself.

Think of it as the difference between a building's floor plan and the furniture arrangement inside each room. The floor plan determines what is possible and what fits where. The furniture arrangement is more flexible, adjusted as needed. Your revised schedule is the floor plan.

This distinction matters for one specific reason: if you confuse the template with the day-planner, you will become frustrated when the week does not follow the template exactly — and you will either abandon it or update it constantly. Neither is useful. The template is meant to survive contact with a real week without needing to be rewritten every time something shifts.

What it does need is a regular, honest review. Specifically:

**Weekly review.** At the end of each week — or at the beginning of the next — spend ten minutes comparing how the week actually went against the template. Where did you follow the structure? Where did it break down, and why? Was the break a genuine exception or a pattern that signals the template needs adjustment?

**Monthly review.** Once a month, look at the template as a whole. Has your life changed enough that some time blocks no longer reflect your situation? Have new commitments appeared that deserve explicit space? Has the business evolved to the point that some activities need more time and others less? Update the template when it no longer fits reality — but not before you have given it enough time to test whether it works.

**First change rule.** Do not revise the template the first time it conflicts with your reality. Real schedules conflict with real weeks regularly. The template's job is not to predict every week perfectly; it is to establish the default structure that most weeks should follow. Give it at least four weeks before making substantive changes.

## Part 2 — Building the Schedule: What Goes In

Your revised schedule should incorporate each of the following:

**Your six anchors.** These are the non-negotiable core. Find a place in the week for each one. Some will require specific, recurring time blocks. Others will be woven into existing activities. All of them should be visible in the final template.

**Your business time commitments from Lesson 3.4.** Each category — client work, business development, administration, learning, and weekly review — should have a designated place in the week, with a specific day and a realistic time estimate. Business development in particular tends to be deferred without a dedicated slot.

**Your personal time commitments from Lesson 3.4.** Physical health, key relationships, daily rest, weekly free time, and the personal activity that keeps you yourself. All of them, explicitly present in the template.

**Your non-negotiable existing obligations.** The things in your current schedule that are genuinely not optional — caregiving, medical appointments, household responsibilities that cannot be delegated.

**One genuine buffer.** Every week has unexpected demands. Build in at least one unstructured time block per week — not as free time, but as overflow capacity. This is not a gap in your schedule; it is a deliberate design choice that prevents one unexpected demand from disrupting the entire week.

What does not go into the revised schedule: the time-wasters you decided to remove. Those time blocks are now available for the commitments above. They do not need to be labeled in the template — simply leave them unscheduled and let the template's other commitments fill them.

## Part 3 — Making the Schedule Live

The revised schedule you have written today has value only if it is in front of you.

Print it out. Write it by hand on a full sheet of paper if that is more meaningful to you. Make copies — one for your workspace, one for wherever you spend most of your waking time at home, one for your car if you are often away from home. Make it a document you encounter, not one you file.

If you use a digital calendar, block out the recurring commitments from your revised schedule this week. Not every specific appointment — just the recurring structure. "Business development, 90 minutes" on Tuesday and Thursday morning. "Walk with spouse, 45 minutes" on Wednesday and Friday afternoon. These blocks are placeholders that protect the structure. Specific appointments fill in around them.

Tell at least one person what your new schedule contains. This does not need to be a formal announcement. It can be as simple as telling your spouse, an accountability partner from the course, or a close friend: "I am protecting Thursday mornings for my business from now on, so I will not be available for anything else during that time." The act of telling someone creates a social accountability that a private document does not.

Review it at the end of this week — seven days from today — against what actually happened. That first comparison is the most instructive one.

## Part 4 — The Connection Back to Your Objectives

Your revised schedule and your five objectives from Lesson 2.5 must be connected.

Look at your five objectives now. For each one, find the behavior you specified — the specific action you committed to taking to achieve the target. Does that behavior have a home in your revised schedule? If it does not, the objective is unlikely to be fulfilled, regardless of how well it was written.

For any objective whose required behavior does not yet appear in the schedule, add it. Use one of the time blocks you designated for business development or client work, or create a specific recurring time block for it if the behavior is distinct enough to warrant one.

This connection — between the what of your objectives and the when of your schedule — is what makes planning operational rather than theoretical.

## Mini-Story — Richard and the Schedule He Actually Used

Richard was sixty-nine, a retired civil engineer. He was methodical, disciplined, and had produced excellent work throughout this module. His current schedule audit was thorough. His six anchors were specific. His time-waster list was honest and complete.

When he built his revised schedule, it was ambitious. Six days of business activity. Networking on three of those days. Learning time every morning. A complete rebuild of his week from the ground up.

His course facilitator looked at it and said: "Would you build a bridge designed for maximum theoretical capacity, or one designed to hold what it actually needs to hold on the worst day you realistically expect?"

Richard rebuilt the schedule. This time he started with the six anchors, added his most essential business commitments, protected his personal time, and left two afternoons genuinely unstructured. The final template was quieter than the first version. It looked, to his slight discomfort, like a schedule that belonged to someone with more patience than drive.

He reviewed it four weeks later. He had followed it on twenty-one of twenty-eight days — a rate he had never achieved with any previous schedule. The three days he had not followed it, he knew exactly why, and he had recovered the missed commitments within the same week.

The schedule he would actually use was not the most ambitious one. It was the most honest one.
