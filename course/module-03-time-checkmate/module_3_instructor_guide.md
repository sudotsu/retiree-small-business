# Module 3 Instructor Guide — Putting Time in Checkmate

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 3**

**For:** Chaz Korb (Instructor)

---

## Module Overview and Instructor Framing

Module 3 is where the course gets practical in a way that some students find uncomfortable. They have spent two weeks doing internal and conceptual work — defaults, big pictures, objectives. Module 3 asks them to look at their actual calendar and make decisions that affect real habits, real relationships, and real obligations.

The tone throughout this week should be honest and supportive in equal measure. Honest about the fact that most people's schedules are not designed — they accumulated. Supportive about the fact that changing them requires patience, not a complete overhaul in a single week.

The two biggest facilitation challenges in Module 3:

1. **Students who resist the current schedule audit** because they have done something similar before in a productivity seminar and found it useless. Acknowledge this directly: the reason this week is different is that it is connected to Modules 1 and 2 — the schedule is being redesigned around who they specifically are and what they have identified as most important, not around a generic template.

2. **Students who build an unrealistic revised schedule** — either too ambitious (every hour scheduled, no buffer, no flexibility) or too minimal (avoids making real commitments). The Richard story in Lesson 3.5 is designed specifically for the former; for the latter, the reflection prompts in Lesson 3.4 tend to surface the avoidance.

## Lesson-by-Lesson Notes

---

### Lesson 3.1 — Write Out Your Present Schedule

**Core concept:** You cannot redesign a schedule you have not honestly mapped. Start with the truth of how the week currently looks.

**Timing:** ~85–90 minutes. The schedule grid takes longer than students expect — especially for people with complex weekly routines. The hour-tally exercise in Exercise 2 adds another 10–15 minutes. Journal runs 15 minutes. This is genuinely one of the longer lessons in the module.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Leaving out habitual activities.** Students frequently omit the two-hour afternoon TV window, the extended phone calls, the social media time. These are often the most significant sources of recoverable time. In community responses, gently note: "What does your day look like between 2 and 5 in the afternoon? That's a common gap in the first draft."
- **Total falling significantly below 168.** If a student's total is 120 or 130, they have significant unaccounted time. Do not treat this as success — it is a gap. Ask: "What fills the hours your total doesn't account for?"
- **Comparing to an ideal rather than the actual.** Some students write what they do on their best week, not their average week. The schedule should represent a typical week — including the slow mornings and the late evenings.

---

### Lesson 3.2 — Default Settings and Big Picture in Your Schedule

**Core concept:** The corporate friend story is the central teaching. A schedule that systematically violates a person's most important defaults costs more than time.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The reading is substantial and should not be skimmed — the lesson's power is in the story and the distinction it makes. Exercises are moderate in length.

**The corporate friend story:** This is derived directly from your Korb source material and is the most personal story in Module 3. If you have a version of this story from your own life or the life of someone you know well — an experience where a schedule worked against someone's core defaults — this is the right place to share it in video or live session. The lesson is written around the story, so your version does not need to be long: two to three minutes is enough.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Choosing anchors that are already in the schedule.** Students sometimes list defaults or big picture goals that already have protected time, making the exercise feel confirmatory rather than challenging. Ask: "Which of these three is most at risk right now — the one getting the least time despite being most important?"
- **Conflating big picture goals with business objectives.** Some students list their Lesson 2.5 business objectives as big picture anchors. Distinguish: the objectives are what you are trying to accomplish; the anchors are the conditions your schedule must maintain to keep you capable of accomplishing them.

---

### Lesson 3.3 — Clearing Space: Eliminating Time-Wasters

**Core concept:** Time is already available. It is occupied by habits and patterns that were never consciously chosen and have never been examined.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The exercise is moderately long, but the real time is in the decision-making — students often sit with the decisions longer than expected. Journal is 15 minutes.

**This is the week's most personal exercise.** Handle community posts with extra care. Some people will share things about their family relationships, habitual behaviors, or personal obligations that are genuinely sensitive. Respond with acknowledgment and encouragement, not advice. The framework (keep/limit/remove) is simple — applying it to real relationships is not.

**The `<INSERT THE KORB STORY HERE>` placeholder in Day 3:** The original source material contains a personal story about eliminating a specific time-waster from your own schedule — one that "almost made you panic" to remove. This is a strong and authentic place for your personal experience. If you have a story like this — a habit, obligation, or pattern you removed from your life that turned out to be recoverable time — share it. The more specific and honest, the better. It normalizes the difficulty of the exercise for students.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Listing only obvious and easy items.** Students who list only their TV time and social media are probably not being fully honest. The harder items — family obligations that no longer fit, long-term commitments to groups or organizations they have outgrown — are where the significant time is. If a student's list contains only digital habits, probe gently.
- **"Limit" decisions that are not actually decisions.** "I will spend less time on the phone" is not a limit decision — it is an intention. "I will not take calls after 8 p.m. and will keep weekday calls to thirty minutes" is a limit decision. Push for specificity in the community.
- **Underestimating recovered time.** Some students remove small amounts from several small items but never address the larger patterns. The total recovered should be substantial enough to make a real difference — at least five to eight hours per week for most students.

---

### Lesson 3.4 — Commit to Time for Business and Yourself

**Core concept:** Both business time and personal time must be explicitly scheduled with equal seriousness. Neither category is optional; neither is earned by the other.

**Timing:** ~75 minutes. The exercises are straightforward if the prior work has been done honestly.

**The personal time commitment is where most students struggle.** Many people in this age group have strong habits of deferring personal needs — health, recreation, relationships — in favor of obligations. The lesson addresses this directly, but your facilitation should reinforce it consistently: scheduling a Tuesday walk or a Friday afternoon off is not indulgence, it is infrastructure. The person your business needs to function is you, and that person requires maintenance.

**Gerald's story** is a good facilitation anchor: the way the Friday afternoon disappeared is a pattern that repeats across many types of commitments. Use it in office hours if needed.

---

### Lesson 3.5 — Your Revised Schedule

**Core concept:** The revised schedule is a working template, not a perfect plan. It is meant to survive contact with a real week and to be reviewed regularly.

**Timing:** ~85–90 minutes. The grid exercise is substantial. The connection to objectives in Part 4 adds meaningful depth but also takes time.

**The Richard story** is useful for students who build over-ambitious schedules. If you see an unrealistic first draft in the community, quote the bridge metaphor directly: "Would you build a bridge designed for maximum theoretical capacity, or one designed to hold what it actually needs to hold on the worst day you can realistically expect?"

**Key facilitation check:** Ask students to verify that every Lesson 2.5 objective behavior has a place in the revised schedule before they mark Lesson 3.5 complete. This connection is the most important thing Module 3 produces and is easy to overlook.

## Suggested Office Hours Topics for Week 3

- "I don't have enough recoverable time to build the schedule I want. What do I do?"
- "My family doesn't understand why I need to protect certain time blocks."
- "My current schedule is so irregular that I can't map a 'typical week.'"
- "I built my revised schedule but I already know I won't be able to keep it."

For all of these: the answer is almost always to start smaller. Protect fewer commitments more firmly, rather than protecting many commitments loosely. A schedule that protects three things completely is more useful than one that protects ten things inconsistently.

## Assessment Notes

Module 3 does not include a formal graded assessment. The deliverable is the revised schedule from Lesson 3.5, verified against the lesson checklist. Key things to confirm:

1. Six anchors are present and marked.
2. Business and personal commitments both appear explicitly.
3. At least one buffer block exists.
4. Every Lesson 2.5 objective behavior has a designated time slot.
5. The schedule looks realistic — not designed for optimal conditions.
