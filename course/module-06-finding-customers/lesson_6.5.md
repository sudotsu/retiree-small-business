# Lesson 6.5 — Your One-Year Lead Generation Strategy

### Estimated Time: Approximately 80–85 minutes

*(Reading: 20 minutes | Exercises: 45 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

*Note: The primary exercise in this lesson — the one-year lead generation calendar — requires cost research before it can be completed. Plan extra time if you have not already looked up costs for the channels on your candidate list.*

---

## Lesson Overview

The work you have done this week — evaluating twelve channels, building a customer profile, tailoring your message, and filtering media against the profile — is only useful if it results in something you actually do.

This lesson produces that result: a written one-year lead generation strategy. Not a wish list, and not a set of good intentions. A plan with specific channels, specific months of activation, and real costs attached.

A written plan does something an unwritten plan cannot: it creates the conditions for accountability. When January becomes March and you have not activated the channel you planned to activate, the written plan makes that visible. You cannot quietly slide past a specific commitment the way you can slide past a general intention.

By the end of Lesson 6.5, you will have:

- A completed one-year lead generation calendar with your top channels mapped against specific months.
- Real or estimated costs for each channel, with a research note for any you have not priced yet.
- A written first-thirty-days action — the specific thing you will do in the next month to begin.

---

## Part 1 — What the Plan Contains

A one-year lead generation strategy for a small business has three elements.

**The channels.** From your filtered list in Lesson 6.4, identify your top three primary channels. These are the ones you are committing to using consistently this year. You may also list one or two secondary channels — ones you will try in a limited way or activate if a primary channel underperforms.

**The calendar.** For each channel, mark the months you intend to be actively using it. Some channels make sense year-round — word of mouth does not have an off-season, and a website is always on. Others have natural timing: direct mail around a seasonal need, a trade show or community event in a specific month, a radio campaign timed to a local high-traffic period. Being deliberate about when you activate each channel prevents the pattern where marketing happens whenever there is time, which is never a consistent schedule.

**The costs.** Write the actual cost for each channel — what it will cost you in money, in time, or both. For any channel where you do not yet know the cost, write a research note and look it up before finalizing the plan. A plan with an unresearched cost is a plan with a hidden assumption that may not survive contact with a real price.

---

## Part 2 — Getting Real Costs

The fastest way to get real costs for the channels on your list:

**Direct mail:** Ask a local print shop for a per-piece price on a simple postcard. Then look up the current USPS bulk mail rate, or ask the print shop if they handle mailing services. A 500-piece mailing to a local zip code is a standard request any print shop can price.

**Newspaper or magazine ad:** Call the publication's advertising department and ask for a rate card. Get the cost per insertion, the minimum run, and whether there is a discount for multiple placements. Most local publications are willing to share this information by phone in five minutes.

**Radio:** Contact the station's advertising or sales department directly. Ask for the cost per thirty-second spot in your target daypart (the time of day most likely to reach your customer). They will also tell you about package deals.

**Facebook advertising:** Facebook's ad manager allows you to set any budget and run a test campaign. A $100 test campaign is enough to learn whether the channel reaches your customers and at what cost per click or inquiry.

**Website:** If you do not have one, ask two or three local web designers for a quote on a simple five-page site. Get the one-time build cost and any ongoing hosting or maintenance fee.

**Word of mouth and referral:** Cost in dollars is near zero. The cost in time — staying connected with referral sources, following up with satisfied clients, asking directly for referrals — is real and should be estimated.

## Exercise 2 — First Thirty Days

A plan that starts "sometime this year" is a plan that starts never. The most important commitment in this exercise is the first one.

Write the specific action you will take in the next thirty days to activate the first channel on your list. Not "I will look into direct mail." Something specific: "I will call Downtown Print by Friday and get a price on 500 postcards to the 43215 zip code." Or: "I will spend one hour on Saturday setting up a Facebook Business page."

## Mini-Story — Ruth and the Plan That Stayed on the Wall

Ruth had been running a pet grooming business out of a studio attached to her house for six years. Her customer acquisition strategy, to the extent she had one, was to do good work and hope her clients mentioned her to their friends. It had produced a steady base of about twenty regular clients — enough to keep her comfortable, but not enough to feel secure.

In the spring of her seventh year, she completed the exercises in this module. She built a customer profile — women 55 to 72, dog owners, concentrated in her township and the two adjacent ones, most of them finding her through the veterinary clinic three miles away. She revised her message. She filtered her channels. She built a one-year plan: three channels, specific months, a total budget of four hundred dollars.

She printed the plan and taped it to the wall above her desk.

By the following spring, Ruth had thirty-one active regular clients and a waiting list for new dogs. The plan had produced the results, but the physical act of putting it on the wall had produced the plan's execution. Every month she could see what she had committed to do and whether she had done it. The months she had not, she could see that clearly too.

Two of her three channels had worked well. One had produced almost nothing. She knew which was which because she had been tracking it. She cut the weak channel and reinvested the money in the one producing the best results.

This is the difference between a strategy and a hope: a strategy tells you what is working.

## Journal Prompts

Take fifteen minutes to write full responses to these questions. Full sentences, not bullet points.

**1.** What do you expect your lead generation strategy to look like twelve months from now — not what you hope, but what you actually expect based on what you know about your capacity, your market, and how long each channel takes to produce results?

**2.** Which channel in your one-year plan are you most uncertain about? What would you need to see in the first sixty days to confirm it is working — or to decide it is not?

**3.** What is the biggest obstacle between your written plan and your actual execution of it? Is that obstacle practical (cost, time, knowledge) or psychological (discomfort, perfectionism, not wanting to seem too salesy)? How will you address it?

**4.** Ruth tracked which channels were working and which were not. How will you track your lead sources? What would you need to put in place to know where a new customer heard about you?
