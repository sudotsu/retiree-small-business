# Lesson 6.1 — The Lead Generation Toolkit

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

## Lesson Overview

"If you build it, they will come."

Only in the movies.

A business without customers is not a business — it is a product waiting in a room. No matter how good the service, how honest the pricing, or how carefully you designed what you offer, the people who would benefit from it cannot buy it if they do not know it exists.

Leads are the people who might become customers. Generating leads is the discipline of making your business visible, consistently, through the channels most likely to reach the people most likely to buy.

This week is about that discipline. Not one magic channel. Not a massive advertising budget. A deliberate, matched strategy — built on who your customers actually are and what you can afford to do consistently.

By the end of Lesson 6.1, you will have:

- A working familiarity with twelve lead generation methods available to a small business.
- An honest evaluation of each method against your specific situation — both its fit and its cost.
- Your top three candidates ranked and reasoned through.

---

## Part 1 — Leads Are a Discipline, Not a Mystery

There is a temptation to think of lead generation as something that either happens or does not — referrals come in, the phone rings, or it goes quiet. This is passive thinking, and it is the most reliable way to end up with an inconsistent, anxiety-producing revenue line.

The businesses that maintain steady customer volume treat lead generation as a system. They know which channels they use. They know approximately what each channel costs in time and money. They know when to lean into one channel and when to pull back. They do not wait and hope — they do specific things on a regular schedule.

You do not need to use all twelve methods on this lesson's list. Most sustainable small businesses use three to five consistently. But you need to know what each one does, what it costs, and whether your potential customers are actually reachable through it — before choosing.

Four things you need to know about your leads:

1. **Who they are** — their profile, which you will build in Lesson 6.2
2. **Where they are** — the places, channels, and communities where they spend attention
3. **How to contact them** — the medium through which they can be reached
4. **How to make them aware of your business** — the message, which you shaped in Module 4 and will refine in Lesson 6.3

Today covers the third item in depth: the channels through which leads can be reached.

## Part 2 — The Twelve Methods

Here is a working overview of the most commonly used lead generation channels for a small business, along with honest notes on what each one does well and where it tends to fall short for the kind of business this course is designed to build.

**1. Word of mouth and referral.** This is the most underrated channel on this list — and the one most business advice books leave out. Most successful small businesses build their core client base from referrals: satisfied clients who tell someone else. It costs almost nothing to activate deliberately, and it produces clients who arrive already trusting you. The risk is over-relying on it without any system behind it — referrals that come passively tend to flow inconsistently.

**2. Newspapers.** Still relevant for local businesses with a local clientele. A well-placed classified or a small display ad in a community paper can reach people who are specifically looking for local services. Less effective for niche or specialized services that are not naturally searched locally.

**3. Magazines.** Effective when creative and well-targeted. Can reach a defined interest audience (hobbyists, professionals, retirees) that aligns closely with your customer profile. Generally more expensive per placement than newspapers, and requires multiple insertions to produce results.

**4. Press releases.** When there is genuine news — a new service, a community involvement, a significant milestone — a press release is a free or low-cost way to get coverage in local papers, community newsletters, or industry publications. Only works if the news is real. A press release announcing "I opened a business" rarely gets picked up; a press release announcing a partnership, an event, or a meaningful community contribution has a better chance.

**5. Telemarketing.** Highly regulated and difficult to execute at scale. However, if you have a specific, permission-based list — contacts who have already indicated willingness to hear from you — a personal follow-up call can be a powerful tool. The emphasis is on targeted and permission-based, not cold.

**6. Direct mail.** Postcards, letters, and printed materials sent to a targeted list. Works best with a defined geographic or demographic target and a specific, actionable offer. Costs more than digital options but reaches people who are not online or who respond better to physical materials — often a meaningful segment of the retiree-audience businesses this course serves.

**7. Trade shows.** Worth considering if your business serves other businesses or a professional community that gathers at industry events. The time investment is significant, but the quality of leads can be high — people who attend trade shows are actively in a buying or decision-making mindset.

**8. Internet — website, social media, and online presence.** Nearly a requirement for any business serving clients who use search engines or social media to find services. A website does not need to be expensive to be effective — a simple, clear page that explains what you do and how to reach you is enough. Social media presence in the right channel (Facebook for some demographics, LinkedIn for professional services) can add visibility at low cost. Email newsletters to an existing list are one of the highest-return digital channels available.

**9. Radio.** Effective when the station can demonstrate it reaches your target demographic in your local area. Works best for businesses serving a broad local audience rather than a narrow niche. Costs vary widely by market.

**10. Television.** Cable and streaming have fragmented audiences into highly specific segments, making targeted TV advertising more accessible than it used to be. The channel-surfing problem is real — a TV ad needs to engage within the first few seconds. Generally higher cost and higher complexity than the other channels on this list; worth considering only if there is a clear match between the audience and the medium.

**11. Billboards, displays, and outdoor advertising.** Strong for daily-frequency impressions with commuters and local traffic. Provides broad local awareness rather than targeted reach. Works well when the goal is name recognition in a defined geographic area rather than direct response.

**12. Telephone directories.** Results vary considerably by business type and location. A basic listing comes with having a business phone number. Whether a paid display ad in a directory produces results depends heavily on whether your potential customers use directories to search — less common for younger demographics, still relevant for some older ones.

## Mini-Story — Phyllis and the Referral That Stopped Referring

Phyllis had built her alterations and tailoring business entirely on word of mouth over the first eighteen months. It had worked well. Her clients were loyal, her quality was consistently excellent, and one satisfied client reliably mentioned her to two or three others. She had never needed to think about marketing because she had never needed to find customers — they found her.

In month nineteen, four long-standing clients moved away within the same three-month window — two relocating for family reasons, two transitioning to care facilities. Her referral flow dropped sharply. She had no other channels activated. She had no presence online. She had not advertised locally. She had not built any community visibility beyond the circle of clients who had been passing her name around.

It took her four months to rebuild to previous volume, entirely by deliberate action: a small Facebook presence that cost nothing to set up, a postcard to her zip code with a discount for new clients, and a request to her remaining clients to refer specifically rather than passively. All three worked. The postcard cost sixty dollars and produced eleven new inquiries.

Phyllis had not done anything wrong in those first eighteen months. She had done something incomplete: she had activated one channel and treated it as a strategy rather than one component of one. The lesson she carried forward was not to abandon referrals — they remained her best source — but to have at least two others running in parallel so that when one slowed, the business did not slow with it.

## Transition to Lesson 6.2

You have evaluated the twelve channels. The next question is: which of your customers will those channels actually reach?

That question requires knowing who your customers are with enough specificity to match a medium to their habits. A channel that reaches a 45-year-old professional on LinkedIn does not reach a 68-year-old retiree the same way. The same service, offered through the wrong medium, produces no leads — not because the product is wrong, but because the audience was not there.

Lesson 6.2 builds your customer profile — the detailed picture of who your typical customer is that will serve as the filter for every channel decision you make this week and beyond.
