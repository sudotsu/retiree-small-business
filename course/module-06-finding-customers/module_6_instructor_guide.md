# Module 6 Instructor Guide — Finding Customers

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 6**

*For Chaz. Not for students.*

---

## Module Overview

Module 6 is the customer acquisition week. Students build a complete lead generation system across five lessons: a channel inventory, a customer profile, a profiled message, a media match analysis, and a one-year written plan. The module is highly exercise-dense — every lesson produces a working document students will keep and use.

The through-line across the module is specificity. Every lesson pushes students away from general intentions ("I'll try social media") toward specific decisions grounded in evidence from their own customer base. Your job as facilitator is to keep that pressure on specificity throughout.

---

## Lesson-by-Lesson Facilitation Notes

### Lesson 6.1 — The Lead Generation Toolkit

**What the lesson does:** Introduces twelve lead generation channels and asks students to evaluate each one for fit and affordability. Students identify their top three candidates.

**Common student patterns:**
- Students often want to use the channels they are already comfortable with rather than the ones that fit their customer. Gently push on this: "Is that channel a match, or is it familiar?"
- The word-of-mouth entry often gets dismissed as "not a real strategy." The lesson addresses this in the reading, but you may need to reinforce it: deliberate, systematized referral-seeking is one of the highest-return activities a small business can do.
- Students with no prior marketing experience may feel overwhelmed by twelve options. Remind them they only need three to five that they execute consistently — the list is for evaluation, not adoption.

**Community thread prompt:** Students share their top three candidates and what makes them uncertain about one. Engagement on these threads tends to be high because business types vary so much — the comparisons are instructive.

**What to watch for:** Students who mark everything as either "yes" or "no" without discrimination. The realistic answer is usually a mix, and students who mark all yes are probably not thinking critically.

---

### Lesson 6.2 — Building Your Customer Profile

**What the lesson does:** Walks students through nine profile questions to build a written Typical Customer Profile. Students then check the profile against their five most recent customers.

**Common student patterns:**
- The most common error is vague answers. "Middle-aged" is not a profile answer. If you facilitate a live session covering this lesson, spend time on the specificity check — have students read their answers aloud and ask whether those answers could narrow a mailing list.
- Students with newer businesses often feel they cannot answer the questions because they do not have enough customers. Encourage honest estimation clearly labeled as such. The profile can be revised as the customer base grows.
- The "last five customers" check at the end of Exercise 2 is the most valuable thing in the lesson. Students who discover a mismatch often have a significant insight at this step.

**Community thread prompt:** Students share the most surprising thing their profile revealed. The stories here are often the most memorable moments of the week.

**What to watch for:** Students who conflate the profile with their target customer rather than their actual customer. Both are useful, but they should be built separately. If the profile describes "who I want" rather than "who has actually been buying," that should be surfaced.

### Lesson 6.3 — The Message to Your Profiled Customer

**What the lesson does:** Students take their core Module 4 message and rewrite it specifically for the customer described in their profile. The objection test identifies the most likely customer hesitation and assesses whether the message addresses it.

**Common student patterns:**
- Many students produce a revised message that is only marginally different from the original. The issue is usually that they changed words but not perspective — they are still writing from the business owner's point of view, not the customer's. Ask: "If the person in your profile read this, would they feel like this was written about their situation specifically?"
- The objection test often surfaces the insight that a message is trying to appeal broadly and is therefore not addressing any specific concern. This is valuable.

**Community thread prompt:** Students share the biggest change between their original and profiled messages, and whether the revised version addresses the most likely objection. Comparing across business types is useful here.

**What to watch for:** Students who do not bring their Module 4 message to this lesson — either because they did not save it or because they have not built one yet. Have them write a current-state message first before revising.

### Lesson 6.4 — Matching Media to Your Customer

**What the lesson does:** Students re-evaluate the twelve channels from 6.1, this time filtered through their customer profile rather than their own preferences. A second column distinguishes Day One channels from channels that build over time.

**Personal story placement — IMPORTANT:** This lesson contains a placeholder (`[KORB STORY HERE]`) after Part 1 for your personal story from your advertising and media background. The story should illustrate matching — or mismatching — a media channel to a specific customer profile. Suggested length: 150–200 words. The Douglas mini-story in the lesson provides a structural parallel (wrong channel for the actual customer) — your story does not need to tell the same story, but it should connect to the same principle. Insert your personal story directly in place of the placeholder before publishing this lesson.

**Common student patterns:**
- Students often resist marking a "familiar" channel as a poor profile match. The habit of using a channel can feel like evidence that it works. Ask: "What in your customer profile actually supports this channel as a match?"
- The Day One / Builds Over Time distinction surprises some students who assumed all marketing works immediately. This is a good moment to align expectations: if they need leads urgently, the plan needs at least one Day One channel.

**Community thread prompt:** Students share the channel that surprised them as a strong match and one they had assumed would work that may actually be a poor fit.

**What to watch for:** Students who mark the same channels as in 6.1 without any change from the profile filter. The profile should change at least some assessments. If it does not, the profile may be too vague to be useful.

---

### Lesson 6.5 — Your One-Year Lead Generation Strategy

**What the lesson does:** Students build a written one-year plan: three primary channels, monthly activation calendar, cost estimates, and a specific first-thirty-days action. This is the capstone exercise of the module.

**Common student patterns:**
- The cost column is frequently left blank or filled with "TBD." Push hard on this: a plan with unknown costs is not a plan. Students should not finalize a channel they have not priced. Give them the concrete research steps from the lesson reading.
- The first-thirty-days commitment is the most important output of the lesson. Students who write vague commitments ("I'll start working on the website") should be pushed toward specific ones ("I'll call three web designers for quotes by [date]").
- Students sometimes try to put all twelve channels into the plan. Remind them: three to five primary channels, executed consistently, will outperform twelve channels done inconsistently.

**Community thread prompt:** Students share their three primary channels and their specific first-thirty-days action with a date. These commitments become a form of public accountability.

**What to watch for:** Students whose calendar is filled with check marks in every month for every channel — that is a wish list, not a plan. A realistic plan has some months of concentrated activity and others of maintenance or rest for certain channels.

## Module Connections

**Module 4 connection:** Students bring their core product message from Module 4 to Lesson 6.3 and revise it for their profiled customer. If students did not produce a message in Module 4, have them write a current-state version before Lesson 6.3.

**Module 5 connection:** The budget work in Module 5 (lesson 5.1 in particular) is relevant to the cost columns in Lesson 6.5. Students who produced a working budget know what they can spend on lead generation. For those who need a reminder: the lead generation budget is an operating expense that belongs in the budget worksheet from Module 5.

**Module 7 preview:** Module 7 picks up where 6 leaves off — what happens after a customer is acquired. The through-line is that finding customers is only half the system; the other half is keeping them long enough to generate referrals and repeat business.

## Assessment Notes

Module 6 produces five working documents (the toolkit evaluation, the profile, the profiled message, the media match table, and the one-year calendar). The richness of those documents is the primary evidence of engagement — a one-line customer profile is not the same as a paragraph built from nine specific questions.

The first-thirty-days commitment in Lesson 6.5 is a good opening question for the start of Module 7: "Did you do the thing you said you'd do? What happened?"
