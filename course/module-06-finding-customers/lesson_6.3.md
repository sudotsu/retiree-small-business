# Lesson 6.3 — The Message to Your Profiled Customer

### Estimated Time: Approximately 70–75 minutes

*(Reading: 25 minutes | Exercises: 30 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

You have a customer profile. You have a core message — the statement of what your business does and why it matters — that you developed or refined in Module 4.

The question now is whether those two things match. Not in theory, but in practice: if the person described in your profile encountered your current marketing message, would it feel like it was written for them?

Most of the time, the answer is not quite. The language is a little too general. The benefit is framed in abstract terms that resonate with the business owner who wrote it but land differently on the customer who reads it. The message is technically accurate and functionally invisible.

This lesson closes that gap. By the end of Lesson 6.3, you will have:

- A revised version of your core product message, written specifically for the customer described in your profile.
- A clear record of what changed between the original and the revised version — and why.
- A message you can begin using immediately in any channel you activate.

---

## Part 1 — The Profile Changes the Language

A message written for everyone uses abstract, broadly applicable language. "Quality service." "We care about our clients." "Tailored solutions for your needs." These phrases are invisible because they do not describe anyone in particular. The person reading them does not recognize themselves.

A message written for your profiled customer uses the specific language of that customer's situation. It references their actual circumstances — not generic circumstances. It frames the benefit in terms of what that specific customer values and what they are trying to resolve.

Three things about your customer profile determine how the message should shift:

**Age and life stage.** A 67-year-old woman navigating retirement is not in the same emotional or practical moment as a 42-year-old mid-career professional. Her concerns, her vocabulary, her decision timeline, and what she is trying to avoid are different. A message that speaks to her speaks to her moment — her specific transition, her specific uncertainty, her specific hope for what comes next.

**Primary motivation.** The profile you built in Lesson 6.2 includes the interests and influences that characterize your customers. Those signals tell you what your customers are moving toward and what they are trying to move away from. A message framed around the right motivation — not a generic benefit, but the one this customer actually cares about — produces recognition. Recognition produces response.

**Language and vocabulary.** Different ages, professions, and communities use different words for the same concepts. The language a 68-year-old retiree uses to describe financial uncertainty is not the language a 38-year-old uses. The language a tradesperson uses to describe quality is not the language a professional services client uses. Match your vocabulary to theirs, not yours.

None of this requires you to invent a new identity for your business. It requires you to describe the same business in language that fits the person most likely to need it.

## Part 2 — The Objection Test

A message is not just what you say. It is whether it addresses the question forming in the reader's mind as they read it.

Every profiled customer carries a predictable first objection. Not a hostile one — simply the instinctive question a person asks before deciding whether to pay attention. For a retiree on a fixed income: "Can I afford this?" For someone who has been burned before by a contractor or service provider: "How do I know this one is different?" For someone who has never used this service before: "Is this really for someone like me?"

Your revised message does not need to answer every possible objection. But it should address the one most likely objection your profiled customer carries — either by removing the friction before it forms or by acknowledging it directly.

This is not about being defensive. It is about showing the profiled reader that you understand their situation well enough to anticipate what they are thinking. That understanding, when it comes through in a message, is itself a form of reassurance.

## Mini-Story — Nancy and the Language Nobody Recognized

Nancy had been running a home organization service for four years. She had built her client base slowly — through friends, through a couple of Facebook posts, through satisfied clients who mentioned her to neighbors. Her business description had always been the same: "I help busy families reclaim their space and reduce daily stress."

It was a reasonable description. Accurate. Clean. It did not produce many calls.

When she built her customer profile, she discovered something she had only half-noticed before: nearly all of her active clients were women between 62 and 75 who were downsizing — clearing out homes that had accumulated forty or fifty years of belongings, often preparing to move or to help an aging parent transition. They were not busy families managing daily chaos. They were people facing a specific, emotionally heavy transition that required practical help and someone who would not rush them.

Nancy rewrote her message: "I help people in the middle of a major move or life change sort through a lifetime of belongings — thoughtfully, without pressure, at your pace."

She did not change her service. She changed the language to match the people who actually needed it. The next postcard she mailed produced three times the response rate of anything she had tried before.

## Journal Prompts

Take fifteen minutes to write full responses to these questions. Full sentences, not bullet points.

**1.** Did your customer profile change how you thought about the message you are using? What specifically was different about the revised version compared to what you had before?

**2.** What is the most likely objection your profiled customer carries — and how confident are you that your revised message addresses it? What might you still need to add?

**3.** Think about a time when a piece of marketing spoke directly to you — when you felt it was written for your situation. What made it land? Does your revised message do any of those things?

**4.** Is there a gap between the customer you currently have and the customer you want to attract more of? If the profile describes who is buying now, what would the message for your ideal target customer look like?
