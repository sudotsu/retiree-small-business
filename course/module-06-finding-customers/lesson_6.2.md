# Lesson 6.2 — Building Your Customer Profile

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 30 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

A criminal profiler does not guess. They study the evidence — the details that connect one crime scene to the next, the behavioral patterns that emerge across multiple incidents — and they build a description that narrows the field from everyone to someone specific.

You need to do the same thing with your customers. Not because they are criminals. Because they have patterns — habits, characteristics, and behaviors that, assembled together, give you a clear picture of who is actually most likely to buy what you offer.

Without that picture, you are marketing to everyone. Marketing to everyone is functionally identical to marketing to no one: your message does not land because it is not aimed at anything specific.

By the end of Lesson 6.2, you will have:

- A written Typical Customer Profile based on nine specific questions about your current and target customers.
- A clear sense of which characteristics most differentiate your ideal customer from the general public.
- A profile you will use in Lessons 6.3 and 6.4 to sharpen your message and filter your media choices.

---

## Part 1 — What a Customer Profile Is and Is Not

A customer profile is a description of your most common, most valuable, or most ideal customer — built from evidence, not assumptions.

It is not a single person. It is a composite built from observation: the characteristics that appear most frequently among the customers who buy, stay, refer, and return. The profile describes the category, not the individual.

It is also not permanent. As your business grows and your customer base shifts, the profile shifts with it. A profiling exercise you complete this week may look different in six months if a new customer segment emerges or an existing one grows. Reviewing the profile annually — or when something in the business changes significantly — keeps it accurate.

The profile does one important thing that assumptions about customers cannot: it forces you to compare what you think is true with what the evidence actually shows. Businesses regularly discover through profiling that their customers are slightly older, or younger, or more concentrated in one part of town, or more interested in a specific benefit than they had assumed. Those discoveries change the channel and message choices in ways that produce better results.

## Part 2 — The Nine Questions

These nine questions produce the building blocks of a usable customer profile. Answer them based on actual experience with real customers — or, for a newer business, on what you have observed so far plus your best-informed estimate of who your target customer is.

Be specific. "Middle-aged people" is not a profile answer. "Adults between 55 and 70, retired or in the last five years of their career, living within 15 miles" is a profile answer.

**1. What media do your customers use?**
Are they on Facebook? Do they read the local newspaper? Do they listen to specific radio stations? Do they use email regularly? Do they search on Google? The channels that reach them are only useful if they are channels those customers actually inhabit.

**2. Who influences your customers?**
Are there people, organizations, or figures whose recommendations carry weight with your customers? A doctor, a financial advisor, a community leader, a church, a local business association, a friend group? Understanding who your customers listen to tells you where you might build a relationship that generates referrals.

**3. What other interests do your customers have?**
Hobbies, habits, affiliations, clubs, causes. These tell you where your customers spend time and attention — which creates opportunities to be visible in the same places.

**4. What is the age range of your customers?**
A ten-year range is more useful than a twenty-year one. The habits, media preferences, and decision-making styles of a 55-year-old and a 75-year-old are often significantly different.

**5. What is the average age?**
Within the range, where do most of your customers cluster? This helps you optimize for the most common case rather than designing for the full range.

**6. Is your customer base more male, more female, or roughly even?**
Gender affects communication style, media preference, decision timeline, and what emotional and practical appeals resonate. Not a judgment — a data point.

**7. What is the approximate income level of your typical customer?**
Not their exact income — a general range that reflects whether your pricing is accessible to them and whether they are making discretionary purchases or addressing more urgent needs.

**8. Where do your customers live?**
A specific geography — zip code, neighborhood, county, region. This determines which local media are worth considering and tells you whether your customer base is concentrated enough for geographic targeting to be useful.

**9. Are your customers mostly from one industry or profession, or from several?**
If your customer base skews toward a specific profession, trade, or life stage, there are likely channels — associations, publications, events — that concentrate those people in one place.

## Mini-Story — Carl and the Profile That Changed the Plan

Carl had been running a personal finance coaching practice for two years, working primarily with adults he thought of as "people approaching retirement" — a broad category he had never tried to narrow further. His marketing reflected that: general financial wellness language, distributed through channels he happened to be familiar with, producing modest and inconsistent results.

When he sat down to build a proper customer profile, the picture that emerged was considerably more specific than he expected. The overwhelming majority of his active clients were women between 58 and 68, recently widowed or divorced, who were navigating financial independence for the first time. They had come to him through the same three sources — a grief counselor referral, a church women's group, and a divorce attorney who occasionally recommended his name. They were not looking for general financial wellness. They were looking for someone patient, non-judgmental, and genuinely familiar with the emotional complexity of major financial transition.

Carl had been marketing to a broad category while serving a narrow one. The profile did not mean he had to stop working with other clients. It meant he could market specifically to the people who were most likely to need him and most likely to stay. He strengthened his relationship with the three referral sources. He changed his website language. He wrote an article for the church newsletter.

His new client intake doubled within four months. Not because he had changed what he did, but because he had finally described who it was for.

## Journal Prompts

Take fifteen minutes to write full responses to these questions. Full sentences, not bullet points.

**1.** Does the customer profile you built today surprise you — is your actual customer different from who you assumed they were? Describe the surprise, or the confirmation, specifically.

**2.** Which of the nine profile characteristics would most change your marketing choices if it shifted — if your customer base aged, or moved geographically, or the income level changed? What does that tell you about which characteristic matters most to your strategy?

**3.** Who influences your customers most — and are you visible to those influencers? If not, what would it take to become visible to them?

**4.** If your typical customer read your current marketing materials, would they recognize themselves in what they read? What would they feel was missing or misaligned?
