# Module 4 Instructor Guide — Marketing: Five P's in a Pod

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 4**

**For:** Chaz Korb (Instructor)

---

## Module Overview and Instructor Framing

Module 4 is where the course shifts from internal work to external practice. Students arrive at Week 4 having built real self-knowledge (Module 1), a clear picture of their life and business goals (Module 2), and a structured schedule designed around those goals (Module 3). This week asks them to apply all of that to the market.

The Five P's framework is deliberately simple. Do not apologize for its simplicity in facilitation — many students who have been through business training before will expect something more elaborate, and some will initially resist what seems like a basic list. The value is not in the framework's complexity. It is in the order of the framework: product before price before propagation before position before promotion. The order matters, and students who jump ahead to promotion without working through the prior four P's will produce hollow promotional materials.

The three biggest facilitation challenges in Module 4:

**1. Students who want to skip to Lesson 4.5 (Promotion).** Promotion is the most familiar-feeling part of marketing for most people — it's the part they've seen. Some students will be eager to write their ads and marketing copy and will rush through the earlier lessons. The community setup (requiring actual community posts for each lesson before the next one opens, if you use the strict prerequisite setting) is the best structural check against this. In facilitation, remind students that a promotional message built without a clear product evaluation, grounded price, and mapped delivery path is like putting a beautiful label on a box before you've filled it.

**2. Students who under-price out of discomfort.** This is the most common emotional obstacle in Module 4 and it surfaces most visibly in Lesson 4.2. Many people in this age group — especially those who came from professions where charging for expertise felt awkward — will arrive at a working price that is below their actual value. Normalize the discomfort and push back gently but directly when you see it in community posts. The Bernard story is designed for exactly this.

**3. Students whose product has a real problem that promotion cannot fix.** The RAW evaluation in Lesson 4.1 occasionally surfaces a genuine product-market fit issue: a product that is not relevant to anyone the student can actually reach, or that has an accessibility problem too fundamental to address without redesigning the offer. If you see this in community posts, handle it privately before the student reaches Lesson 4.5. Promotion is not the answer to a product problem, and spending a week building a promotional plan for a product that needs to be redesigned is a waste of the student's time and enthusiasm.

## Lesson-by-Lesson Notes

---

### Lesson 4.1 — Your Product Through Your Customer's Eyes

**Core concept:** The gap between how a business owner sees their product and how the customer sees it is where most marketing problems begin. The RAW framework (Relevance, Accessibility, Worth) gives a structured way to evaluate that gap.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The two-part exercise — customer's perspective, then owner's perspective — is what takes time. Students who try to rush through it produce superficial answers. In community facilitation, if a student's RAW analysis lists only strengths and no weaknesses, probe gently: "What would a customer who considered your product and chose something else have said?"

**The "baby problem" opening.** This is the right tone for the entire module — honest without being harsh, self-aware about the bias that comes with building something. Use it to set the expectation that this week requires a particular kind of uncomfortable honesty.

**Key facilitation watch:** Students who conflate their own view with their customer's view throughout both exercises. The purpose of doing Exercise 1 (customer view) before Exercise 2 (owner view) is to force a distinction. If both exercises produce identical answers, the student has not actually borrowed the customer's perspective — they have answered from their own perspective twice. Ask: "If you told three customers your biggest RAW weakness, would they agree — or would they name something different?"

---

### Lesson 4.2 — Pricing: Making Value and Price Meet

**Core concept:** The bungee cord metaphor frames the week's pricing lesson: price and value must be consciously brought together — they do not meet automatically. The three-step process gives a grounded method.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The exercise is moderately complex — three steps, specific numbers required. Students who arrive without basic cost awareness (what it actually costs them to deliver their service per hour or per unit) will stall at Step 2. If you see this in the community, point them toward making their best estimate now and refining it later, rather than leaving Step 2 blank.

**The `<KORB STORY HERE>` placeholder.** The Day 2 source material for this module includes a `<KORB STORY HERE>` placeholder where your personal story about pricing belongs. This is a high-value facilitation moment. If you have a story from your own business experience about the tension between what you charged and what your work was actually worth — a moment when you set a price that felt too high and discovered it was not, or when you under-priced yourself and paid the cost — this is the right place for it. It does not need to be dramatic. It needs to be honest and specific.

The format: share it in the lesson video introduction (if recording one), or open your live session with it before students discuss their own pricing work.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Working price at the bottom of the range with no justification.** "I'll start low and raise prices when I have more clients." This is the under-pricing trap with a rationale attached. It almost never works as planned — low initial pricing tends to attract clients who are shopping on price, which makes it harder to raise rates later without losing them. Challenge it.
- **Missing overhead in Step 2.** Many students calculate the low extreme based only on time cost and miss recurring overhead: software subscriptions, materials, vehicle wear, professional memberships. The low extreme without overhead will be higher than they expect when they include it — which is usually clarifying.
- **Treating the working price as permanent.** Some students are reluctant to name a number because they worry it locks them in. Emphasize that this is a working price — the best estimate available at this stage, subject to revision when real market data is available.

### Lesson 4.3 — Propagation: How Your Product Reaches Its Customer

**Core concept:** Every step between a business and a paying customer has a time cost, a money cost, and a friction cost. The right propagation path for a small business is the one that is most accessible to the specific customer at a delivery cost the business can sustain.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The propagation map is the most time-intensive exercise — students who think through their delivery chain carefully will fill out more rows and spend more time on each one. The connection to the Module 3 schedule is a genuinely important check that many students will not have made on their own.

**The Module 3 schedule connection.** Make this explicit in facilitation: the hours a student's delivery path requires are not free hours. They are hours that need to exist in the revised schedule from Lesson 3.5. A student who maps a delivery chain requiring eight hours per week of in-person delivery but allocated only five hours for business operations in their schedule has a mismatch that will show up as exhaustion or missed commitments within two months. Surface it now.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Under-counting the steps.** Many students write "deliver the service" as a single step without breaking down what that actually involves. How does the customer book? Who sends the confirmation? How is the session set up? How is it delivered? How is follow-up handled? How is payment processed? Each of these is a step with a time cost. Encourage specificity.
- **Defaulting to a delivery channel that matches their comfort, not their customer.** Helen's story is the anchor for this pattern. Use it directly in community facilitation if you see it.

---

### Lesson 4.4 — Positioning: What Lives in Your Customer's Mind

**Core concept:** Positioning is what already exists in the customer's mind about a business — intentional or not. Managing it deliberately requires understanding where you currently sit and closing the gap between that and where you want to be.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The position analysis table requires that students know enough about their competitors to evaluate them honestly. Students who have no competitors to name yet should use the two most plausible alternatives their target customers might consider.

**The connection to Modules 1 and 2.** Part 3 of this lesson makes an explicit argument: the most authentic and sustainable positioning is built from the default settings and big picture that already emerged in the first two modules. In live sessions or video, develop this. A student who knows their core values from Module 1, their strengths from Module 2, and their purpose clearly enough to have written five business objectives — that student has a foundation for positioning that most of their competitors do not. Most competitors have never asked themselves who they actually are. That clarity is a competitive advantage, and it is one that cannot be easily copied.

**Frank's story.** The home inspector narrative is specifically designed for students who have been positioning themselves as affordable by default. Use it to normalize raising prices as a positioning decision, not just a financial one.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Rating all five elements "Strong" or "Average" without differentiation.** Push for honesty: "Which of the five is your weakest right now, if you're being fair to yourself?"
- **Writing a desired position that sounds aspirational rather than honest.** "The best home inspector in the region" is not a position — it is a hope. "The only inspector with a structural engineering background" is a position. Push for specificity and truth.

---

### Lesson 4.5 — Promotion: Your Message and How It Travels

**Core concept:** Promotion is the synthesis of the prior four P's, expressed as a message and delivered through channels where the customer actually makes decisions. Done before the other P's are in place, it is hollow. Done after, it is the amplifier that brings everything together.

**Timing:** ~80–85 minutes. This is the longest lesson in the module. The message architecture exercise is genuinely demanding — converting product understanding into benefit language, choosing the right price signal, writing an actionable propagation call-to-action, and positioning in a single sentence. Students who shortcut this will produce generic messages. The first draft advertisement is where quality shows.

**The draft advertisement community post.** This is the most valuable community exercise in Module 4. A student who posts a real draft — imperfect, honest, for their actual product — and receives feedback from peers is doing real marketing work. Facilitate this generously. When you comment on drafts, focus on:

- Does it lead with benefits or features? (Most first drafts lead with features.)
- Does it say what the customer should do next? (Many first drafts omit the call to action.)
- Does it say something that a competitor in the same category could not say? (Most first drafts are generic enough to apply to any business in the field.)

**Patricia's story.** The retired marketing executive who spent her budget on the wrong channel is specifically designed for students who have professional marketing experience and are inclined to default to the channels they already know. The point is not that their expertise is useless — it is that the customer's behavior matters more than the marketer's comfort.

**The product warning.** If any student's honest Lesson 4.1 RAW evaluation surfaced a real product problem and it has not been addressed — this is the last point at which to raise it before they build a full promotion plan. A strong promotional message built on a weak product foundation is the most efficiently harmful thing a small business can do to itself.

## Suggested Office Hours Topics for Week 4

- "My RAW evaluation revealed that my product has an accessibility problem I don't know how to solve."
- "I can't figure out what to charge — every number I pick feels either too high or too low."
- "I don't have any real competitors yet. How do I do the position analysis?"
- "I wrote my draft advertisement and it sounds like everyone else in my field. How do I make it distinct?"
- "My delivery path is too expensive and I can't figure out how to simplify it without reducing quality."

For all of these: the answer is almost always to go back to the specific P that is causing the friction and work through it more honestly than the first time. The frameworks in this module are self-correcting — if an answer is not producing a useful result, the problem is usually insufficient honesty at the step that produced it.

## Video Content Notes

If recording lesson videos for Module 4 (6–8 minutes per lesson suggested):

- **Lesson 4.1:** Open with the baby analogy — tell it directly and warmly. The tone should be "I know you love what you've built, and this week is about seeing it the way your customer does."
- **Lesson 4.2:** Open with your personal pricing story. The `<KORB STORY HERE>` placeholder is specifically for this. If you have a moment from your own business life where you had to reconcile what you were charging with what your work was actually worth, this is where it belongs.
- **Lesson 4.3:** Walk through your own product's propagation path as an example. Make it real — show what the actual steps are, including the ones that cost time or create friction.
- **Lesson 4.4:** Use Frank's story as a direct reference and connect it explicitly to the Module 1 and Module 2 work. "You already know who you are and what you value. That's your positioning foundation."
- **Lesson 4.5:** The most important thing to say here is the warning about promotion amplifying what is already there. Say it plainly: "Before we talk about where your message goes, make sure the message is true and the product behind it is real."

## Assessment Notes

Module 4 does not include a formal graded assessment. The deliverables are:

1. A completed RAW evaluation with both customer and owner perspectives and a gap analysis.
2. A working price with documented reasoning — not just a number.
3. A propagation map with step-by-step delivery chain and time/cost estimates.
4. A position analysis table and one clear desired-position sentence.
5. A completed message architecture and a first draft advertisement in real language.

Key quality checks:

- The working price from Lesson 4.2 should be inside the documented pricing range and above the cost floor.
- The propagation map's time total should be reconcilable with the student's Module 3 revised schedule.
- The desired position sentence from Lesson 4.4 should be specific and honest — not aspirational.
- The first draft advertisement should lead with benefits, include a clear call to action, and say something the student's competitors could not credibly claim.
