# Lesson 4.2 — Pricing: Making Value and Price Meet

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 35 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

In Lesson 4.1 you evaluated your product honestly through both your customer's eyes and your own. You found the gap — the specific dimension of Relevance, Accessibility, or Worth where the two views diverge most.

Worth is where pricing lives. And pricing is where a lot of excellent small businesses quietly undermine themselves.

The challenge is not that business owners do not know their prices. They do. The challenge is that setting a price is not the same as setting the *right* price — one that accurately reflects the value your product delivers and that your customer's mind will recognize as fair. Getting price and value to meet in the customer's mind is the central problem of pricing for any small business, and the one most commonly bypassed with a number pulled from instinct.

By the end of Lesson 4.2, you will have:

- Understood the distinction between price and value and why closing the gap between them is marketing's core pricing challenge.
- Worked through a three-step process to set a price that is grounded in your product's real value, your cost realities, and your competitive context.
- Identified the specific pricing error you are most at risk of making — and what to do about it.

## Part 1 — The Bungee Cord Problem

There is a particular frustration that comes with bungee cords and luggage racks. The cord looks long enough. It feels like it should reach. You stretch it to what seems like the limit — and it comes up an inch short. Every time. So you grunt, you adjust, you lean into it, and somehow it works. But it never feels like it should be this hard.

Every business has the same challenge with pricing. Price and value need to meet in the mind of your customer — and they almost never meet automatically. Price is what you charge. Value is what the customer believes they are getting in exchange. Both are real. But they are measured differently, by different people, in different terms.

You measure price in dollars. Your customer measures value in the relief of a solved problem, the pleasure of something they love, the confidence of a decision made well. The bungee cord is always slightly too short to connect those two measurements without effort.

This is not a failure of your product. It is the nature of pricing. The three-step process in this lesson is how you do the work of closing that gap deliberately rather than hoping instinct gets you close enough.

## Part 2 — What Retirees Get Wrong About Pricing

Before the three steps, it is worth naming the two specific pricing errors that appear most often among people entering business for the first time after a career working for someone else.

**The under-pricing trap: charging less than your worth out of discomfort.**

This is the most common pricing error for people who have spent careers in professions where charging felt awkward — teachers, nurses, social workers, engineers who were always on salary. The logic sounds like humility: "I do not want to overcharge anyone. I just want to help and make a little money." But under-pricing creates three real problems.

First, it signals to customers that your product is less valuable than it is. Price communicates quality. A price that seems surprisingly low does not make customers grateful — it makes them suspicious. Why is it so cheap? What is wrong with it?

Second, under-pricing makes your business unsustainable. You can only help your customers as long as your business stays alive. A business that charges too little does not stay alive.

Third, under-pricing is often a way of avoiding the discomfort of putting a real number on the value of your expertise. But your expertise is the product. If you do not believe it is worth a real price, you will have a hard time convincing a customer that it is.

**The isolation trap: pricing based on what you think seems fair without checking the market.**

The second trap is subtler. You decide what you would be willing to pay for something and use that as your pricing anchor. But you are not your customer. Your sense of what is fair may be based on your own income, your own spending habits, your own generation's relationship with money — none of which may match your actual customer's frame of reference.

Market pricing is not about copying your competition. It is about knowing what your customer's alternatives cost so that your price exists in a real-world context, not in a vacuum.

## Part 3 — Three Steps to a Price That Works

Work through these three steps in order. Each one builds on the one before it.

**Step 1: Start with what makes your product valuable to your customer — and what they might be willing to pay for it.**

Not what it cost you to develop. Not what you want to earn per hour. Start with the customer's experience: what specific problem does your product solve, or what specific pleasure does it provide? What would the customer's life or business look like without it?

Then estimate: given that value, what range of prices would a reasonable customer in your market consider and not immediately reject? This is a rough estimate based on your honest understanding of your customer, not a calculation.

**Step 2: Establish your price extremes.**

The high extreme: the most you could realistically charge for your product given what competitors charge for comparable products. Above this price, customers will reliably choose an alternative.

The low extreme: the least you could charge and still sustain your business. This requires you to account for your actual costs — your time, any materials, overhead, and the minimum income you need from this work to make it worth continuing.

The gap between your high extreme and your low extreme is your pricing range. Your final price lives somewhere inside it.

**Step 3: Set a price that matches the customer's perceived value and keeps your business alive.**

The right price is not at the top or bottom of your range. It is the point where these two things are both true: the customer's internal calculation of worth lands at or above the price, and your business survives at that price.

This price may feel uncomfortably high compared to what you imagined charging. That discomfort is usually the under-pricing trap showing up on schedule. Name it, acknowledge it, and set the price anyway.

This is not your permanent price. Markets shift. You will learn more about your customers. You will revise. But you need a price to start, and it should be a real one — not a convenient number that lets you avoid the discomfort of valuing your own work.

## Mini-Story — Bernard's Tax Work

Bernard had spent thirty-two years as an accountant. When he retired, a handful of neighbors started asking him for help with their tax returns. He charged them twenty-five dollars. It felt right — more than nothing, less than anything that felt like real money, and easy for everyone to say yes to.

Word spread and he started getting more calls. He said yes to most of them, and the work started taking over his Mondays and Tuesdays from January through April. He was busy, not quite satisfied, and consistently exhausted by the end of tax season.

One April a neighbor he had never worked with asked for his rate. When he said twenty-five dollars, she paused. "That seems very low," she said. "Is that for a basic return?"

He had been charging twenty-five dollars for returns that took him two to four hours — returns a professional service in town charged one hundred fifty to two hundred dollars for. He had set his price based on what felt comfortable to ask for, not on what his work was worth.

He raised his rate to one hundred fifty dollars the following January, expecting to lose clients. He lost two. He gained six new ones — people who had previously assumed that his low price meant he was not very good. His Monday and Tuesday workload dropped by a third, his income from tax work nearly quadrupled, and he had enough margin in his schedule to actually enjoy the work again.

The product had not changed. The price signal had.

**1.** Which pricing trap — under-pricing out of discomfort, or pricing in isolation from the market — are you more at risk of? What specific belief drives that risk? Write out the belief as plainly as you can.

**2.** After completing the three-step exercise, is your working price higher or lower than what you originally expected to charge? What, specifically, changed between your starting instinct and your final number?

**3.** If your working price is higher than what you are emotionally comfortable charging — what would a customer need to see, hear, or experience in order to find that price not just acceptable but obvious? Write about the presentation of the product, not the product itself.
