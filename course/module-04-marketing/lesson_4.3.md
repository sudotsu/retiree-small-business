# Lesson 4.3 — Propagation: How Your Product Reaches Its Customer

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 35 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

You have a product that passes the RAW test and a price grounded in real value and real costs. The next question is one that is easy to overlook until you try to make your first sale: how does your product get from where it starts to the person who wants it?

This is not a logistics question. It is a marketing question, and a significant one. The path your product takes to reach your customer shapes who can reach it, how much it costs to deliver, how long it takes, and how the experience of receiving it reflects on you and your business.

This lesson covers the third of the Five P's: Propagation — the delivery channels through which your product or service moves from your business into the hands of your customers.

By the end of Lesson 4.3, you will have:

- Understood what propagation means and why it matters as a distinct marketing consideration.
- Mapped the specific steps your product takes from your business to your customer, including estimated time and cost at each step.
- Identified whether your current propagation plan is the right one for your product and your customer — and where it can be simplified or improved.

---

## Part 1 — The Newspaper Problem

For decades, newspapers worked out a specific delivery system for getting their product to readers. They printed the paper at a central facility. Trucks took it to regional distributors. Distributors divided it by route. Carriers delivered it to individual addresses before dawn. The whole chain had to work correctly — every day — or the product never reached the customer.

Then the internet arrived. A newspaper could now deliver its entire product to any reader in the world at the moment of publication, with no trucks, no distributors, no carriers, and no dawn runs. The delivery cost dropped to nearly zero and the potential reach expanded to everyone with a connection.

Not every business made that transition. But every business has had to ask the same underlying question the newspaper had to ask: what are all the steps between where my product starts and where my customer receives it — and is that the right path?

That question is what propagation is about.

---

## Part 2 — What Propagation Really Means

Propagation is not just distribution. It is the complete answer to the question: how does your product reach your customer?

That answer includes:

**Where the transaction happens.** Does your customer come to you — to a physical location, a website, a phone number? Or do you go to them — to their home, their office, the event or platform where they already are?

**Who is involved in the path.** For some products, it is just you and the customer. For others there are intermediaries: a referral source, a platform, a retail store, a fulfillment service. Every person or step in that chain adds time, cost, and a point where something can go wrong.

**What the customer experiences during delivery.** The moment your product arrives — physically or digitally — is a marketing moment. It shapes what the customer thinks of you before they have even fully used what they bought.

**What it costs.** Propagation costs money, time, or both. A service delivered in person requires your physical presence, which is both a cost and a constraint. A product shipped requires packaging, postage, and handling time. A digital product has its own delivery infrastructure costs, which are much smaller but not zero.

Not all delivery paths are available to all products. A home nurse cannot deliver their service online. A software developer can. A landscaper cannot do their work remotely; a writer can. Understanding what your product actually requires — and what your customer actually needs — is the work of this lesson.

---

## Part 3 — Matching Channel to Product and Customer

The right propagation path for your business is the one that makes your product as accessible as possible to the specific customers you are trying to reach — at a delivery cost your business can sustain.

Four questions help you find it:

**1. Does your product require physical presence — yours, your customer's, or both?**

Services like tutoring, home repair, caregiving, and fitness training require some form of physical presence. Products like physical goods require shipping or pickup. Understanding whether physical presence is required, optional, or irrelevant determines which delivery channels are actually available to you.

**2. Who is your customer, and where are they when they are most likely to want what you offer?**

A retired schoolteacher offering online tutoring to middle school students reaches her customers where they already are — on devices, through their schools and parents, on platforms parents already use. A retired chef offering cooking classes to adults in her neighborhood reaches her customers at a community center or in her home kitchen. The channel must exist where the customer already is, not where you prefer to operate.

**3. How many steps between you and your customer are necessary, versus how many are habits or defaults you have not examined?**

Every unnecessary step in a delivery chain is a point of friction — for you and for the customer. If you can cut a step without reducing the quality of the customer's experience, cut it.

**4. What is the true cost of your delivery path — in time as much as money?**

Remember your Module 3 schedule. The hours your propagation system requires are hours from your revised template. If your delivery plan requires you to be in a car or at a shipping counter three afternoons a week, those afternoons are already allocated. If that does not match what your schedule can support, you need a different propagation path — or a different product format.

## Mini-Story — Helen's Referral Problem

Helen had spent thirty-five years as a clinical pharmacist before she retired. She understood medication interactions the way most people understand their own kitchen — intuitively, fluently, and with a depth that took decades to build. When she decided to offer medication management consulting to seniors — reviewing their prescriptions, flagging potential interactions, and explaining their medications in plain language — she assumed the clients would come from their doctors.

She spent the first two months introducing herself to three local physicians. She dropped off brochures. She followed up. One physician seemed interested and said he would mention her to patients. A month later, no referrals had come through.

The problem was not her product. The problem was her propagation assumption. She had built a delivery path that required doctors to interrupt their workflow to refer patients to an outside consultant they had never worked with — and she had built no easy mechanism for them to do it. There was no referral form, no clear process, no shared documentation.

She stepped back and thought about where seniors dealing with medication complexity actually were. Not in doctors' offices, waiting for a recommendation. They were at senior centers, at church, at family dinners talking to their adult children. She contacted two local senior centers about speaking to their members. She printed a simple one-page handout explaining what a medication review was and who it helped. She asked her existing clients — the handful she had through personal connections — if they would share her name.

Within six weeks she had more inquiries than she could comfortably schedule. The product had not changed. The delivery path had.

**2.** The lesson connects propagation to your Module 3 schedule. Where does your delivery path consume the most time? Is that time already allocated in your revised schedule, or does it represent unplanned demand that needs to be addressed?

**3.** Think about the last time you bought something and found the delivery or acquisition process frustrating — a step that felt unnecessary or confusing. What did you do? Apply that experience to your own delivery path: what is the one step where you most risk losing a customer who wanted to say yes?
