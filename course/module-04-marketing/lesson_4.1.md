# Lesson 4.1 — Your Product Through Your Customer's Eyes

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

## Lesson Overview

You have spent three weeks building internal infrastructure: who you are, what you want, and how your week is structured to support it. This week, the course turns outward.

Marketing begins with a simple but frequently underestimated reality: your product — whatever it is you are building and selling — does not exist in a vacuum. It exists in the minds of the people you are asking to pay for it. And the picture those people have of your product is not automatically the same picture you have. The gap between those two pictures is where most small business marketing problems begin.

This week covers the five components of marketing that matter for a small business owner: your product, your price, how your product reaches customers, how it is positioned in their minds, and how it is promoted. The framework is called the Five P's — and today begins with the first one.

By the end of Lesson 4.1, you will have:

- Evaluated your product honestly against the three criteria your customers use to make buying decisions — relevance, accessibility, and worth.
- Identified the specific strengths and weaknesses of your product from your customers' perspective.
- Compared that view with your own perspective on your product and named the gap between them.

## Part 1 — The Baby Problem

There is a known truth about newborns that parents learn quickly: every parent who carries a baby home believes they are holding the best-looking child on the block. And they are not wrong — in their own way. But they are also not objective. Newborns are compared enthusiastically by their parents and evaluated quietly by everyone else.

Your product is your baby. You have spent months, possibly years, developing it — your skills, your offer, your business. You have shaped it, refined it, worried about it. It is genuinely yours, and your feeling about it is genuine. But that feeling is not your customer's feeling.

Your customer does not compare your product to how long you worked on it. They do not weigh it against your personal investment in it or the difficulty of building it. They compare it to everything else available to them that solves the same problem. They compare it automatically, often without thinking consciously about it. And they are not polite in that comparison the way people are polite about babies.

This is not a reason for discouragement. It is the first thing you need to understand about marketing: the purpose of everything in this module is to bridge the gap between how you see your product and how your customer sees it. You cannot bridge a gap you have not honestly measured.

Today you measure it.

## Part 2 — The RAW Framework

The three criteria your customers use to evaluate your product can be organized into a single acronym: RAW.

**R — Relevance**

Relevance is whether your product actually addresses something your customer wants or needs.

This sounds obvious. It is not as easy as it sounds. Many small business owners build a product around what they are good at, or what they enjoy, or what they have always done — and assume that a customer will find it relevant. That assumption is sometimes right, but it is a shortcut over the most important question in marketing: what is your customer actually trying to accomplish?

A customer does not buy a lawn service because they want their lawn mowed. They buy a lawn service because they want to stop worrying about their lawn, or impress their neighbors, or reclaim a Saturday afternoon. If you describe your business as "mowing lawns," you are describing your process. If you describe it as "giving you your Saturday back," you are describing what is relevant to the customer.

Relevance is not just about whether your product exists — it is about whether your product speaks directly to what is actually going on in the customer's life.

**A — Accessibility**

Accessibility is whether your customer can get to your product with reasonable effort.

Accessibility is not just about where your product is sold. It includes:

- Discovery — can they find you? Do they know you exist?
- Simplicity — is it easy to understand what you offer in the first thirty seconds?
- Ease of purchase — is buying from you simple, or does it require multiple steps, phone calls, or confusing forms?
- Ease of delivery — once they buy, does getting the product into their hands feel effortless or frustrating?

A product can be deeply relevant and still fail on accessibility. If the right person cannot find you, cannot understand quickly what you offer, or finds the buying process confusing or effortful, they will move on. Not because they do not want your product — because getting it costs them more energy than they are willing to spend.

**W — Worth**

Worth is whether your product delivers enough value to justify its cost.

Worth is not the same as price. Price is what you charge. Worth is the calculation your customer makes — consciously or not — when they weigh your price against what they expect to get in return. A $400 service that solves a problem they desperately need solved is worth it. A $40 service that addresses something they could easily do themselves is not, regardless of how low the price seems to you.

Worth is also shaped by everything surrounding your product: how it is presented, how you communicate about it, the reputation you carry, and the experience of working with you. Two businesses offering identical services can be perceived as having completely different worth — not because of what they do, but because of how they present and communicate what they do.

## Part 3 — The Gap That Matters

When you finished Exercise 1, you were seeing your product through a borrowed lens — asking yourself what your customer would say, not what you would say. That is a different and harder question.

Now do the same exercise from your own perspective.

Not to contradict what you wrote in Exercise 1. To find where the two views diverge.

The gap between your view of your product and your customer's view of it is not a problem to be fixed by pretending the customer is wrong. It is the map of the work ahead. Every marketing decision you make this week — how you price, how you distribute, how you position, how you promote — is ultimately an attempt to close specific parts of that gap.

Some gaps are small and easy to close. A customer who cannot find you can be reached with better visibility. A customer who does not understand your offer can be reached with clearer communication. A customer who is not sure your product is worth the price can be reached with more compelling demonstration of the value it delivers.

Some gaps are large and require a harder look at the product itself. If your honest assessment of the customer's perspective reveals that what you are offering does not actually address something they want or need — that is not a marketing problem. It is a product problem, and no amount of promotion will solve it. The source material for this course says it plainly: all the promotion in the world will not mean a thing if the product does not hold up.

This is not said to discourage you. It is said so that the work you do this week is aimed at the right problems.

## Mini-Story — Dorothy's Jam

Dorothy had been making jam for thirty years. Her strawberry preserves had won two county fair ribbons and her apricot-ginger was the jar people asked about by name. When she retired from teaching, she decided to sell it.

She set up a small table at a local farmers market on Saturday mornings and spent the first month mostly explaining what was in each jar. Sales were slow and inconsistent. She asked a few regular customers what they thought of the product. Every single one of them loved it. So she asked a harder question: why hadn't they told more people?

The answer surprised her. "I didn't know how to get more once the market closed," one of them said. "I came back the Saturday after I first tried it and you weren't there."

Dorothy had been missing alternate Saturdays due to a standing commitment. She had not thought to mention this, or to put a card in the bag with a phone number, or to take email addresses for a small order list. The jam itself was not the problem. The gap was entirely in accessibility.

She printed a simple half-page insert for every bag: a list of which Saturdays she would be there, a phone number for orders, and a note that said "pre-orders welcome." Within six weeks, she had a standing list of twelve customers who ordered in advance regardless of whether they came to the market.

The product had not changed. The customer's experience of accessing it had.
