# Module 1 Instructor Guide — Knowing Yourself

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 1**

**For:** Chaz Korb (Instructor)

---

## Module Overview and Instructor Framing

Module 1 is the most internally demanding week of the course. Students are not building anything visible — no website, no product, no marketing plan. They are examining themselves. That can feel unproductive to action-oriented people, and some will push back on it, either explicitly or by skimming the exercises.

The week works because of what it prevents. Students who do the Module 1 work honestly build businesses with far fewer self-sabotage patterns later. Students who skim it hit those same patterns in Module 3 or 4 and do not know why things feel stuck. The investment pays out several weeks down the road.

The practical framing that helps: "This week you are building the operating system that runs your business decisions. Everything you build in Modules 2 through 8 runs on it."

## Lesson-by-Lesson Notes

---

### Lesson 1.1 — What Really Drives Your Decisions

**Core concept:** Every business decision is filtered through default patterns — invisible, automatic, and usually unexamined. Naming the top three decision drivers is the first concrete act of self-awareness in the course.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The Decision Influences Drill (Exercise 1) takes longer than most students expect — the instruction to list 21 items before editing tends to slow people down. Reassure them that speed matters more than quality on the first list; they will refine it.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who list values but avoid the uncomfortable ones.** The drill asks for fears and habits alongside the positive influences, but students who want to present a good version of themselves will default to listing respectable items (faith, family, purpose) and skip the friction-producing ones (fear of judgment, financial anxiety, need for approval). The journal prompts are designed to surface what the drill left behind — particularly prompt 2 (fear disguised as realism) and prompt 3 (the people who see limitations more than potential).

- **Students who say their top driver is "family" and stop there.** "Family" is rarely one driver — it usually contains multiple competing forces. The person who says "my family drives everything" often means "my spouse's approval matters more than my own judgment" or "I need to prove something to my adult children." Encourage specificity in office hours or community replies.

---

### Lesson 1.2 — Mapping Your Default Settings

**Core concept:** The four categories — Attitudes, Beliefs, Motivations, People — provide a structured way to inventory the inner landscape. The goal is observation, not judgment.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The Default Settings Map (Exercise 1) is the most important exercise in the module. Students who invest thirty minutes on it have cleaner material to work with in Lessons 1.3 and 1.4. Students who rush it get stuck later.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who mark everything "Helps me."** This is avoidance. If a student arrives at the Lesson 1.3 Plus/Minus tables with nothing marked "Sometimes hinders me" or "Usually hurts me," the analysis has no material to work with. If you see this in a community thread post or office hours, a useful question is: "Which of your defaults has ever caused a problem — even a small one — in a business or work situation?"

- **Students who struggle with the People category.** Naming a spouse, child, or close friend as "draining" feels disloyal. The framing in the lesson ("This is data, not judgment") is intentional. Reinforcing it in community posts matters — particularly for students who have family members who are skeptical of the course. The Lesson 1.2 community thread is often where this tension first surfaces, and how it is handled there sets the tone for how honestly students engage in Lessons 1.3 and 1.4.

---

### Lesson 1.3 — Positives and Negatives: The Reality Check

**Core concept:** Every default has both a positive and a negative dimension. Strengths flip under pressure. The flip patterns, not the defaults themselves, are what guardrails are designed to manage.

**Timing:** ~85–90 minutes. This is the longest lesson in the module. Four Plus/Minus tables take real time to complete honestly. Students who rush the tables get superficial guardrails in Lesson 1.4 that do not hold. If students are splitting it across two sessions, the break between Part 1 (tables) and Exercise 2 (reframes) is the natural stopping point.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who confuse "Negative" with "character flaw."** The lesson defines Negative as "creates friction for business goals under pressure" — not a moral judgment. Students who take the analysis personally may shut down or produce overly self-critical responses. Redirect: "This is a performance analysis, not a character assessment. A race car that understeers on wet pavement isn't a bad car — it's a car with a known behavior you account for."

- **Students who cannot identify flip conditions.** The flip observation exercise ("when does this strength become friction?") trips up students who have not experienced a business failure or a significant stall. Useful prompt: "Think of a time in your career when a strength of yours caused a problem — when being thorough, or being kind, or being decisive created a mess. What were the conditions?" Most people can find one if the question is concrete enough.

---

### Lesson 1.4 — Rewrite Your Defaults and Set Guardrails

**Core concept:** Beliefs and attitudes are not fixed. They can be updated deliberately. Guardrails are the behavioral structures that hold the new beliefs in place while they take root.

**Timing:** ~75–80 minutes. The Defaults Map Version 2 can run long if a student has many items flagged "Usually hurts me." Students with four or five significant rewrites should budget ninety minutes.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who write inspirational rewrites that they do not believe.** A rewrite that sounds good but does not feel true will not hold. The most common example: "I am too old to learn technology" rewritten as "Technology is easy and I love learning it." That is not honest for most students. A more useful rewrite is something like: "I can learn one tool at a time. I have figured out harder things before." The test: can the student say it out loud without a skeptical internal response?

- **Students whose guardrails are too abstract.** "Be more consistent" is not a guardrail. "I do one hour of business work before noon, Monday through Friday, no exceptions" is. Push for specificity in community thread feedback. The guardrail exercises in the lesson are designed to produce specific behaviors — reinforce that standard in your replies.

---

### Lesson 1.5 — Fit Check and Decision Gate

**Core concept:** "Ready" does not mean perfect or fear-free. It means current conditions are aligned well enough with what a sustainable business requires to make forward movement viable.

**Timing:** ~70–75 minutes. This is the integration lesson — shorter reading, structured assessment, a signed document.

**Common student patterns:**

- **Students who rate themselves Green out of optimism rather than honesty.** A Green rating without genuine support in the Time, Money, and Support categories means Module 2 is being built on a shaky foundation. If a student's community thread post reads like a pep talk rather than an honest assessment, the Fit Statement exercise is where to look — a genuine Green rating produces a specific, grounded Fit Statement. An optimistic one produces vague language.

- **Students who rate themselves Red and stop there.** Red is not a stop sign; it is a re-route. If a student posts a Red rating in the community thread, the most useful response from Chaz (or a community moderator) is not reassurance but a specific question: "What is the one thing that would need to change for you to feel Yellow? What is the smallest version of that change you could make this week?"

- **Students who skip the Fit Statement.** The signed, dated Fit Statement is the most important deliverable of Module 1. Students who reach Module 2 without it tend to lose their grounding point when the work gets harder. If a student marks Lesson 1.5 complete without posting a Fit Decision in the community thread, they likely did not complete the statement.

## Community Thread Topics for Week 1

- Lesson 1.1: "Post one of your three starred decision drivers — what it has helped with, and where you plan to watch for it."
- Lesson 1.2: "Share one pattern that shows up in more than one category for you — and what it means for your business."
- Lesson 1.3: "Share one Positive you are going to build on and one Negative you are working to reframe — with the specific behavior that keeps it in place."
- Lesson 1.4: "Post your rewritten default about [topic] and the guardrail you added."
- Lesson 1.5: "Post your Fit Decision (Green / Yellow / Red) and the one thing you are watching most carefully going into Module 2."

The Lesson 1.5 thread is the most diagnostically useful thread of the week. The range of responses there tells you a great deal about the cohort's readiness — and where to focus attention in Module 2's first office hours session.

---

## Assessment Notes

Module 1 has no graded assessment. The deliverables are:

1. The completed Default Settings Map (Lessons 1.2–1.3) — a private document in the student's workbook.
2. The Defaults Map Version 2 and guardrails sheet (Lesson 1.4) — private, but referenced in the community post.
3. The signed, dated Fit Statement (Lesson 1.5) — the primary accountability marker for the module.
4. The Lesson 1.5 community thread post — the public accountability marker.

Students who have their Fit Statement and a Lesson 1.5 community post visible are well-positioned for Module 2. Students missing both should be checked in on before they advance.
