# Lesson 1.3 — Positives and Negatives: The Reality Check

### Estimated Time: Approximately 85–90 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 45 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

By now you have identified your decision drivers and mapped your default settings across Attitudes, Beliefs, Motivations, and People.

This lesson is where the work gets specific and practical: every default setting carries both a positive dimension and a negative one. Optimism attracts opportunity — and sometimes underestimates effort. Caution reduces risk — and sometimes blocks movement. The aim is not to fix your personality. It is to understand it clearly enough to design a business structure that amplifies what helps and manages what does not.

Today you will:

- Run a structured Plus/Minus analysis on all four default categories.
- Identify the patterns that tend to surface when you are under pressure — the places where strengths flip into friction.
- Draft the guardrail ideas you will finalize in Lesson 1.4.

> **Note:** This is one of the longer exercises in the module. The four Plus/Minus tables take time to complete honestly. Allow the full 85–90 minutes.

---

## Part 1 — What "Positive" and "Negative" Actually Mean Here

In this lesson, "positive" does not mean a trait that looks good on a personality profile. It means a trait that helps you reach your business goals.

"Negative" does not mean a character flaw. It means a trait that creates friction for those same goals — particularly when you are tired, rushed, or under pressure.

This distinction matters because it keeps the analysis practical instead of personal.

*Example — Attitude: "I jump in quickly."*

- Positive: You move ideas into action fast. You test and learn without overthinking.
- Negative: You may skip planning steps that prevent costly rework.

*Example — Belief: "If I am kind, business will come."*

- Positive: You build trust and loyalty. Customers like working with you.
- Negative: You may underprice, overgive, and burn out — because the belief makes it difficult to draw boundaries.

You are not judging who you are. You are evaluating how specific settings perform inside a business context.

## Part 2 — Spotting the Flip Under Pressure

Defaults shift when you are under pressure. That is why some weeks feel smooth and others spiral — and why the same trait can be an asset on Tuesday and a liability on Thursday.

Scan your four tables and ask:

- Which Negative appears in more than one category? *(If the same type of friction shows up under both Beliefs and Attitudes, it has more reach than you may realize.)*
- Which Positive has a clear tipping point — a condition where it flips into a problem? *(Generosity flips when you give away work. Thoroughness flips when it becomes perfectionism.)*
- When do these flips happen most — at certain times of day, around specific tasks, after particular kinds of conversations?

*Pattern examples:*

> "My optimism is an asset for starting things. It flips into underestimation when I skip the planning step."

> "My security motivation keeps my finances stable. It flips into avoidance of marketing when bills feel tight, because marketing feels risky."

Write these flip observations down. They become the inputs for your guardrails in Lesson 1.4.

## Exercise 2 — The Three-Step Reframe

Choose one Negative from each of the four categories — four Negatives total.

For each one, complete the three-step reframe:

**Step 1 — Name it clearly.** No softening, no hedging.
*"I avoid pricing conversations because talking about money makes me tense."*

**Step 2 — Extract the value underneath it.** Every persistent default, even the ones that cause friction, is protecting or serving something. What is it?
*"I care about fairness. I do not want to seem greedy or transactional."*

**Step 3 — Redirect it into a specific habit.** This is the guardrail idea — a concrete behavior that honors the value while preventing the friction.
*"I will write my prices down and review them aloud once a week, so I can say them calmly in a real conversation."*

Write all four reframes. Keep each one short and honest.

## Part 3 — Draft Your Guardrail Ideas

Before Lesson 1.4 — where you will finalize these — draft at least three guardrail ideas now. They should be short, specific, and visible.

A guardrail is not a resolution. It is a practical rule with a clear condition.

*Examples:*

- **Time guardrail:** "I work one hour per day on the business, Monday through Friday, before lunch. Not two hours one day and nothing the next."
- **Money guardrail:** "I price using a written sheet. No custom discounts without sleeping on it first."
- **People guardrail:** "I ask one person for encouragement and a different person for advice. I do not mix those roles."
- **Over-giving guardrail:** "Fifteen minutes of free help. After that, a paid path or a clear no."
- **Perfection guardrail:** "One pilot with five clients beats three more weeks of research."

Write three of your own in your workbook. They do not need to be final. They need to be honest.

## Mini-Stories

**Mary — Kindness With a Limit**

Mary's belief was: *"Kindness builds business."* It was accurate. But it was also costing her.

She overgave. She took calls that ran long and did not charge for them. She discounted when clients pushed back, not because the price was wrong but because she felt guilty. The belief that was attracting clients was also draining her.

Her reframe: *"Kindness includes clarity. Fifteen minutes free, then a paid next step."*

Within a month: more referrals, more paid work, less resentment. The belief did not change. The behavior around it did.

**John — Caution With a Timer**

John's caution protected his savings and prevented costly mistakes. It also meant he had been "almost ready to launch" for nine months.

His reframe: *"Research for ninety minutes, then one real-world test."*

He stopped preparing indefinitely. He started learning through contact with actual customers. The quality of his decisions improved because they were made on real data, not theoretical research.

Both Mary and John built guardrails that honored the value of their default while limiting its reach. Neither of them changed who they were.

## Journal Prompts

Take fifteen minutes to write full responses to these questions. Full sentences, not bullet points.

**1.** Which Negative pattern costs you the most in terms of time, energy, or opportunity? Describe a specific recent example of it costing you something.

**2.** Which Positive, if you doubled it — with a clear boundary to prevent the flip — would produce the most forward movement in the next thirty days?

**3.** What conditions tend to flip your strengths into friction? Is it a time of day, a type of task, a specific kind of conversation, or something else? The more specific your answer, the more useful it becomes.

**4.** What would tell you in thirty days that your new guardrails are working? Describe one concrete, observable sign for each guardrail you drafted.
