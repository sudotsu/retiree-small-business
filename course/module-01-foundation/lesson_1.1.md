# Lesson 1.1 — What Really Drives Your Decisions

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 30 minutes | Exercises: 30 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

Every business decision you will make in the coming weeks begins long before spreadsheets and marketing plans.

It begins in your defaults — the invisible rules that have guided every "yes" and "no" you have made for decades. Most people have never written these rules down. They simply operate on them, assuming they work, noticing only when they do not.

This lesson uncovers those rules so that the business choices you make from here forward fit who you actually are — rather than working against you.

Honesty matters more than speed here. Completeness matters more than perfection.

By the end of Lesson 1.1, you will have:

- Identified the specific influences — people, values, habits, fears — that shape how you make decisions.
- Named the top three that affect your most significant choices.
- Compared how those influences showed up in a decision that worked and one that did not.

---

## Part 1 — The Story Behind Your Defaults

Think of a time you made a significant life decision — buying a house, changing jobs, moving, raising children, retiring.

You probably did not flip a coin.

You weighed things: comfort against risk, security against freedom, what your gut said against what the numbers said. You considered what certain people would think. You filtered options through values you may not have been able to name at the time.

That weighing process is your decision driver system. It runs quietly, consistently, and usually without examination.

If you want a sustainable business in this chapter of life — one that fits your energy, your relationships, and your sense of purpose — understanding that system is not optional. It is the foundation everything else rests on.

---

## Exercise 1 — Decision Influences Drill

**Goal:** Surface the forces that are actually shaping your choices right now.

**Step 1:** Set a timer for five minutes. List every influence you can think of that might affect an important decision you make — people, values, habits, fears, beliefs. Aim for at least 21. Go fast and do not edit.

*(Examples: spouse's opinion, financial security, fear of failure, desire to help others, faith, what my children might think, wanting to stay useful, physical health.)*

**Step 2:** Cross out five items that matter least to you today.

**Step 3:** Circle your top seven from what remains.

**Step 4:** Put a star next to the three that most affect your major life choices — the ones that show up in the room even when you have not invited them.

**Step 5:** Write one sentence for each of the three starred items:

*"When I make big decisions, this influence shows up by \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_."*

---

## Part 2 — Pattern Recognition

Look at your top three. These are not random. They are a pattern that has been operating in your life for a long time.

Some of these influences have served you well. Some have probably been a source of friction — particularly in decisions where they pulled against each other, or where acting on one of them came at a cost to another.

Patterns are the real curriculum of this module. By the time you finish Week 1, your emerging business foundation will be shaped by what you discover here. The guardrails, the business structure, and the schedule you protect will all grow from what you see today.

---

## Exercise 2 — Decision Replay

Choose one major decision from the past ten years that turned out well, and one that did not.

Use this two-column format in your workbook or journal:

| Decision | Key Influences That Were Active | Outcome and What You Learned |
|----------|---------------------------------|------------------------------|
| The one that worked | (List your top three drivers that were present) | (What made it work) |
| The one that struggled | (List your top three drivers that were present) | (What you would adjust) |

After filling in both rows, write three sentences about what the comparison tells you — specifically about how your defaults behaved in each situation.

---

## Mini-Story — Evelyn's Security Driver

Evelyn had spent forty years in corporate HR. Her top decision driver was security. It showed up everywhere: in how she negotiated, how she managed her savings, how she responded to uncertainty.

When she retired, that same driver nearly stopped her from starting the downsizing consulting service she had been thinking about for years. Every time she got close to a real commitment, the security driver found a new reason to wait.

Once she recognized it clearly — named it, not as a personality flaw but as a decision pattern — she stopped arguing with it and started working with it. She launched with two clients per month instead of ten. Security stayed honored. Progress happened anyway.

That is what self-awareness in a business context actually looks like. Not overcoming your defaults — designing around them.

## Journal Prompts

Take fifteen minutes to write full responses to these questions. Full sentences, not bullet points.

**1.** Which of your three starred influences shows up in both your successful decisions and your difficult ones? What does that tell you about it?

**2.** How often does fear disguise itself as being realistic in your decision-making? Can you name a specific example from the past year?

**3.** Who in your life influences your major decisions most — and do they tend to see your potential or your limitations? How does that shape what you bring to them?

**4.** What is one small change in how you approach a decision that would help you feel more deliberate and less reactive?

**5.** If someone who knew you well described your decision-making process, what would they say? Is their description accurate? Is it how you want to be described?
