# Day 3 — Putting Time in Checkmate

### Time: 65–75 minutes
*Reading and reflection: 20 min / Exercise: 35 min / Journal: 10 min*

---

## Overview

You have a picture of where you want to go. Now you need to deal with the thing that determines whether you ever get there: time.

Today is a schedule exercise. Not a motivational conversation about work-life balance — a real, practical reconstruction of how your week is organized. You will look honestly at what currently has your time, what needs your time, and what you can eliminate. Then you will rewrite the schedule.

This is harder than it sounds. Most people who have been retired for any length of time have filled their hours with things that feel necessary. Some of them are. Some of them are habits. Today you find out which is which.

---

## Why This Matters

Running a business on an unexamined schedule is like trying to drive without knowing how much fuel you have. You may get somewhere. You may not. And when you run out, you will be surprised — even though the gauge was available the whole time.

A business needs predictable time. Not necessarily large blocks — many successful small businesses were built in ten-hour weeks. But predictable time: hours that are set aside, protected, and actually used for business work. Without that structure, the business runs on whatever is left over after everything else, which is usually very little and very scattered.

There is a particular challenge for retirees. When you were working, your schedule had a backbone — a job with hours that organized everything else around it. Retirement removes that backbone. For some people, this is liberating. For others, it creates drift. Time expands to fill whatever space it is given, and suddenly there is no time for anything because there is no structure for anything.

The schedule you build today is not a day-by-day appointment book. It is a template — a master plan that shows where your time commitments live across a typical week. From this template, your detailed calendar (paper or digital) will flow. Think of it as the architecture, not the furniture.

A few things worth deciding before you fill in the grid:

**What are your non-negotiable commitments?** These are the things that must be on the schedule regardless of the business — medical appointments, family obligations, physical activity, spiritual practice, whatever keeps you functional and connected. If they are not on the schedule, the business will eventually crowd them out.

**What are your time-wasters?** Every person has them. Some are obvious (two-hour television stretches that you do not particularly enjoy). Some are less obvious (phone calls with people who reliably take more than they give). Name yours honestly.

**What does the business actually need?** Think through the core activities — customer contact, production or service delivery, administration, marketing. Estimate how much weekly time each one requires. Then assign it to the schedule, not as aspirational blocks but as real commitments you will keep.

The goal is not a perfectly optimized schedule. The goal is a schedule that is honest, sustainable, and actually reflects what matters.

## Frank's Story

Frank was seventy-one and had been retired for three years when he started a small tax preparation service for other retirees in his neighborhood. He had been a CPA for most of his career and could do the work in his sleep.

The first tax season was chaos. He had clients calling at all hours, files spread across the kitchen table, and his wife navigating around his paperwork to cook dinner. He told himself this was just what the first season looked like and it would settle down.

It did not settle down. The second season was worse because he had more clients.

The problem was not volume. It was that Frank had never established when the business existed and when it did not. He had no schedule, which meant the business had all of his time by default.

After the second season, he sat down and built a template. Business hours: 9 AM to 1 PM, Monday through Thursday, January through April. Phone calls returned during those hours only. No files on the kitchen table after 1 PM.

The third season he had his best revenue, his calmest household, and four afternoons a week free. His wife noted that she had gotten her husband back. Frank noted that the schedule had not limited the business — it had made the business possible to keep.
