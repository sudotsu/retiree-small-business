# Day 6 — You Need Leads to Succeed

### Time: 65–75 minutes
*Reading and reflection: 20 min / Exercise: 35 min / Journal: 10 min*

---

## Overview

You have a business and a financial picture. Now you need to answer the question that determines whether any of it goes anywhere: where are your customers going to come from?

A lead is a person who might become a customer. Lead generation is the discipline of finding them, making contact, and letting them know your business exists. Today you build the foundation of that discipline — not a complete marketing campaign, but an honest assessment of which channels are available to you and a commitment to the one you will act on this week.

---

## Why This Matters

More good businesses fail from insufficient lead generation than from bad products or poor service. The business works fine — there just are not enough people finding out about it.

This happens for a predictable reason. Most people who start a business are energized by the thing they do well: the craft, the service, the expertise. Finding and attracting customers is a different kind of work — more uncomfortable, less familiar, often feeling uncomfortably like selling yourself. So it gets deferred. And then deferred again. And then you have a business that is technically capable of delivering excellent results and very few people to deliver them to.

The good news is that lead generation does not have to be complicated or expensive. For a small business, a few well-chosen channels done consistently will outperform a dozen channels done sporadically. The question is not how many things you are doing. It is whether what you are doing is reaching the right people and doing it often enough to matter.

There are four things you need to know about every lead:

**Who they are.** Not "anyone interested in what I do" — that is not a lead, that is a population. A lead is a specific type of person: their situation, their problem, their reason for being in the market for what you offer.

**Where they are.** Where do these people spend their time, both physically and digitally? This determines which channels are worth using.

**How to contact them.** What is the path from them existing to them hearing about you? Is it a referral from someone they trust? A presence in the places they already look? A direct approach?

**How to make them aware.** Once you can reach them, what do you say? This connects directly to the positioning work you did on Day 4. A clear positioning statement is the core of every lead generation message.

Twelve channels are listed in the exercise below. Some will be right for your business. Some will not. The exercise asks two simple questions about each: Would you actually use it? Can you afford it right now? Then you commit to one action this week.

That last part matters. The purpose of this exercise is not a list of possibilities. It is a decision followed by an action.

## A Note on Word of Mouth

The exercise lists twelve channels. Word of mouth is first on the list because it is the most underestimated and consistently most effective channel for small businesses, particularly service businesses.

A referral from someone a potential customer trusts is worth more than any advertisement you could run. The customer arrives already half-convinced. They already have reason to trust you before you have said a word.

This does not happen automatically. It requires that you deliver work worth talking about, that your satisfied customers actually know you want referrals, and that you make it easy for them to refer you. These three things together constitute a referral strategy. Many businesses have the first element and forget the other two.

If you have existing customers and are not actively asking for referrals, that is the most effective and least expensive lead generation change you can make today.

## Rita's Story

Rita started a resume and job-search coaching service after retiring from thirty years in human resources. She had the expertise and a strong professional network. She spent her first two months building a website, designing a logo, and getting her LinkedIn profile in shape.

At the end of month two she had one client — a friend who felt obligated.

She had been preparing to be found. She had not been generating leads.

A former colleague suggested a different approach. Instead of waiting for people to discover her website, Rita identified twenty-five people in her network who worked in industries where mid-career transitions were common. She sent each of them a personal note — not a mass email, a personal note — explaining what she was doing and asking if they knew anyone who might benefit.

Within three weeks she had seven clients. Her website had not changed. Her rates had not changed. The only thing that changed was that she stopped waiting and started asking.

She still has a website. It has never been her primary source of clients. Her referral network has.
