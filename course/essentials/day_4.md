## Overview

Today you build the marketing foundation for your business. Not a full marketing plan — that comes with more time and more data. What you build today is the message: what your business is, what it costs, how it reaches customers, and what makes it worth choosing over everything else available to them.

These are the questions every business eventually has to answer. Doing it now, even imperfectly, is far better than discovering two years in that you have never actually articulated why someone should choose you.

---

## Why This Matters

Marketing has a reputation for being complicated and expensive. That reputation mostly benefits the people who sell marketing services. For a small business, the fundamentals are straightforward — and getting them clear is the single most effective thing you can do before you spend a dollar on promotion.

The framework here uses five words that start with P, which makes them easier to remember and easier to check your work against.

**Product** is what you are actually selling — not the description in your head, but the version a stranger would understand. Can you explain what you do in two sentences to someone who has never heard of you? If not, that is the first thing to fix. Customers cannot buy what they cannot understand.

**Pricing** is what you charge and, more importantly, why you charge that. Pricing communicates value. A price that is too low signals that the product is not worth much. A price that is too high without a clear reason will cost you customers who would have bought at a reasonable price. You do not need a final price today — you need a pricing rationale.

**Propagation** is how your product gets from you to the customer. Do they come to you? Do you go to them? Do they order online and receive it by mail? Do you deliver? The answer shapes your entire operation, so it deserves careful thought. The most common mistake here is assuming the customer will figure it out. They will not. You need to make the path to purchase completely clear.

**Positioning** is what makes your product distinct from everything else a customer could buy to solve the same problem. This is not the same as saying you are better. Better is a claim everyone makes. Positioning is specific: you serve a particular type of person, or you solve a particular version of the problem, or you do it in a way nobody else does. Strong positioning does not require mentioning competitors — it simply makes your business the obvious answer for the right customer.

**Promotion** is how the public learns about your business — and it is the only P that involves spending money, which is why it should come last. Once you know what you are selling, at what price, through what delivery method, and positioned how, then you can make smart decisions about where and how to promote. Promoting before the other four Ps are clear is one of the most expensive mistakes a small business can make.

## Vivian's Story

Vivian had run a catering operation out of her home for two years before she realized she had no idea why people hired her instead of the three other caterers in her area. She had customers, but she could not tell you what was bringing them in.

A friend who had worked in advertising sat down with her and asked one question: "Who is your customer, exactly?"

Vivian said, "Anyone who needs catering."

Her friend shook her head. "That is not a customer. That is a population. Who specifically?"

After an hour of conversation, what emerged was this: Vivian's actual customers were families planning retirement parties and milestone celebrations for people over sixty. She had been doing this for two years without knowing it. Her food was good, but what her customers were really buying was someone who understood that seventy years of life deserved a certain kind of celebration — and who treated the guests of honor with the dignity they had earned.

That was her positioning. It had been true the whole time. She had just never said it.

Once she could say it, her promotional materials changed, her referrals increased, and she stopped competing for corporate lunches she was never going to win. She was not the right caterer for every event. She was exactly the right caterer for the ones that mattered to her customers most.
