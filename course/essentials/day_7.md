## Overview

Yesterday you built a strategy to find customers. Today you build the strategy to keep them.

These are different disciplines. Getting a customer requires visibility and a compelling offer. Keeping a customer requires something harder to systematize: a relationship. Today you think through how your business handles the customer relationship at every stage — from first contact through long-term loyalty.

This is where most of the value in a small business actually lives.

---

## Why This Matters

The economics of customer retention are straightforward and often surprising: it costs five to eight times more to acquire a new customer than to keep an existing one. A customer who buys from you repeatedly, refers others, and gives you honest feedback when something goes wrong is worth far more than their first transaction. That accumulated value is what the business community calls lifetime value — and most small businesses never stop to calculate it.

Consider what that number looks like in practice. A customer who buys from you twice a year at $200 per transaction, over ten years, is worth $4,000. If your average customer fits that profile, and you have twenty such customers, you are looking at $80,000 in lifetime value from people you already have. The instinct to constantly chase new customers, while underinvesting in the relationships you have already built, is one of the most expensive habits a small business owner can have.

Keeping customers is also relational work. And while that phrase sounds soft, the mechanics are concrete.

**Turning leads into customers** requires a clear, low-friction path from "I am interested" to "I have purchased." What does that path look like in your business? How long does it take? Where do people drop off? Every obstacle between first contact and first purchase is a customer you did not keep.

**Customer service that earns loyalty** is not about being pleasant — it is about being reliable and responsive. Customers who feel seen and handled well become regulars. Customers who feel like a transaction, or who hit friction when something goes wrong, go somewhere else and tell people about it.

**Lifetime value** is a frame that changes how you think about every customer interaction. If the person standing in front of you is potentially worth $4,000 over ten years, the investment in handling their situation well looks different than if they are worth a single $200 sale.

**Turning best customers into advocates** — people who actively recommend you to others — is not accidental. It requires giving your best customers information they can share (what you do, who you serve best, what makes you different), treating them in a way that makes them want to tell others, and occasionally asking them directly. Most people who would refer you have not been asked.

**Staying on customers' radar** means maintaining a presence in their awareness between purchases. You do not need a sophisticated marketing campaign for this. A personal note, a check-in call, a piece of useful information relevant to their situation — these maintain the relationship without requiring a transaction.

## Dorothy's Story

Dorothy ran a small alterations and tailoring shop out of a space attached to her home. She had been at it for seven years and had a steady stream of customers. But she had never given much thought to any individual customer's history with her business. People came in, she did good work, they paid and left.

Her landlord raised her rent significantly in year eight, and she had to either raise her prices or move. She raised her prices by fifteen percent.

Three of her twenty regular customers did not come back. She did not know why. She had never taken their contact information, never followed up after a job, and had no way to reach out.

She was also sitting on something she had never calculated: her average regular customer came in four times a year and spent roughly $85 per visit. Over seven years, that was $2,380 per customer. She had never looked at that number. When she did, losing three customers felt different than it had before.

Dorothy started keeping a simple log — name, contact, last visit, what they brought in. She began sending a brief personal note to customers she had not seen in sixty days. She started telling people with specific alteration needs that she specialized in exactly that kind of work and asked if they knew anyone who needed the same.

She recovered two of the three lost customers within a year. She gained eleven new regulars in the next two years, almost all through referrals. The rent increase stopped being a crisis.

None of it required a marketing budget. It required paying attention to the relationships she had been leaving on the table for seven years.
