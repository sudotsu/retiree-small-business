## Why This Matters

There is a well-documented difference between goals that live in your head and goals that are written down. The written ones get done at a substantially higher rate. This is not motivational folklore — it reflects the simple reality that writing a goal forces specificity, specificity creates accountability, and accountability is what separates intention from action.

The objectives you write today follow a specific format for a reason. Each objective must contain three elements:

**What** — the outcome you are committing to. Not "improve my marketing" but "acquire three new clients in the landscaping sector." Specific and measurable.

**How** — the action that will produce the outcome. Not "work harder" but "attend two local business networking events per month and follow up with every contact within 48 hours." Concrete and executable.

**By when** — a date. Without a date, a goal is a wish. The date creates urgency and gives you a point at which to honestly assess whether you did what you said you would.

You will write three objectives: one personal, one about profits, and one about promotion. These three categories are not arbitrary — they represent the three most common areas where small business owners let intentions remain intentions.

A personal objective is one about your life outside the business: health, family, relationships, faith, recreation. It is here because the business is meant to support your life, not replace it. If there is no personal objective, the business has no accountability to the person running it.

A profit objective is about the financial health of the business: revenue targets, expense reductions, cash flow improvements, budget adjustments. The numbers from Day 5 are the starting point.

A promotional objective is about how customers find you: a specific lead generation action, a referral strategy, a marketing initiative with a measurable result attached.

## Walter's Story

Walter finished a course very much like this one and did what most people do: he put the workbook on the shelf and told himself he would come back to it.

He came back to it eight months later, when the business had drifted in directions he did not fully understand. Looking at what he had written at the end of the course, he could see almost immediately where the drift had started. He had not acted on two of his three priorities. His promotional objective had never been touched. His personal objective — to keep two evenings per week free for his family — had been abandoned by month three.

None of this was a surprise when he looked at the page. It was all there, in his own handwriting. He had known what needed to happen. He had simply not kept the document anywhere he would see it.

He rewrote his objectives, printed two copies — one for his desk and one on the refrigerator — and told his wife what he was committing to so that someone other than himself knew.

Six months later he checked both copies. He had done what he said he would do on seven of nine items. The two he had not completed had new target dates.

The business did not change because of the document. The document changed because he treated it as a working tool instead of a finished product. That is the difference between a plan that helps and a plan that sits.
