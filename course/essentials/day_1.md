# Day 1 — Your Business and Your Default Settings

### Time: 60–70 minutes
*Reading and reflection: 20 min / Exercise: 30 min / Journal: 10 min*

---

## Overview

Before you build anything, you need an honest answer to one question: Are you actually wired for this?

That is not a discouraging question. It is the most useful question you can ask at the start of a business, and almost nobody asks it. They jump to the product, the name, the website — and then six months in they discover that running a business requires things from them that they did not know they would be required to give.

This course starts differently. Day 1 is a gut check. You are going to look at your default settings — the internal wiring that shapes how you respond to pressure, uncertainty, and opportunity — and decide whether those settings support a business right now.

---

## Why This Matters

The concept of default settings comes from computing: the manufacturer's configuration that stays in place unless you actively change it. Your defaults work the same way. They are the attitudes you lead with before you think about it, the beliefs you hold about what is possible for someone like you, the motivations that actually get you out of bed, and the people whose opinions shape your decisions.

None of these are permanent. But most people do not examine them, so they operate on settings they inherited or accumulated without choosing. A retiree who grew up in a household where self-employment was considered reckless carries a default that works against a business — unless they know it is there and have decided to override it.

The goal today is not to judge your defaults. It is to see them clearly. A default setting that will not support a business is not a character flaw. It is information. You can work with it, change it, or decide the timing is not right. What you cannot do is run a business successfully on defaults you have never examined.

There are four categories worth assessing:

**Attitudes** are your emotional posture toward situations before you have had time to think them through. Under pressure, do you move toward problems or away from them? When something does not go as planned, is your first instinct to diagnose or to blame?

**Beliefs** are what you hold to be true about yourself, about business, and about what people like you are capable of. If you believe that successful business owners are a different kind of person than you are, that belief will undercut every effort you make.

**Motivations** are what is actually driving you — not what sounds good when someone asks, but what genuinely gets you moving. Money is a legitimate motivation. So is proving something to yourself. So is leaving something behind. Know what yours is, because motivation is what carries you through the long stretches when nothing is working.

**People** are the individuals whose reactions and opinions you factor into your decisions. Some of them will support a business. Some will not. Some will be neutral until they see how serious you are. You need to know who is in each category before you start, not after you have made commitments.

## Nora's Story

Nora spent twenty-three years as a hospital administrator before retiring at sixty-six. Within three months she knew she was not done working — she had too much energy and too many opinions about how healthcare facilities could run better. A consulting business seemed obvious.

What stopped her for almost a year was a belief she had never examined: that consultants were people who had worked at big firms, had credentials on their walls, and charged rates that real organizations could afford. She was just a retired administrator from a regional hospital system.

A friend finally asked her what she would tell a hospital director who said they could not afford outside help. Nora gave a ten-minute answer without pausing for breath. Her friend said, "That. That is your consulting practice."

The belief that stopped her was not true. It was a default she had absorbed from watching a certain kind of consultant work. Once she saw it clearly, she could examine it honestly — and find out it did not hold up.

She has been consulting for four years. Her clients are mid-size regional systems, the exact organizations she spent her career in. The credential they needed was not a name on a door. It was someone who had done the job.
