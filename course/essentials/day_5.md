## Overview

Today you face the numbers. Not projections, not plans — actual numbers. What is currently flowing into your business and out of it, and whether those two things add up to a business that can sustain itself.

This day has two parts. Part one is cash flow: a three-month picture of actual cash in and out. Part two is a budget: planned revenues and expenses, and what the net result looks like.

Most people find one of these two exercises uncomfortable. Do it anyway. An honest financial picture — even an ugly one — is one of the most useful things you can have at the start of a business. A flattering picture that does not reflect reality is one of the most dangerous.

---

## Why This Matters

There is a distinction in this course between cash flow and profit that is worth understanding precisely, because confusing them is one of the most common ways small businesses fail.

**Profit** is what the books show. It is the difference between your revenues and your expenses on paper. A business can be profitable on paper and still close because it cannot pay its bills.

**Cash flow** is actual money — the cash you have on hand and in the bank on a given day. If your revenues are real but your customers are slow to pay, if you have stock you paid for but have not sold, if your expenses are due before your income arrives — you can be technically profitable and still unable to cover payroll, rent, or the invoice that is due on Friday.

This is not a theoretical problem. It is the most common reason healthy-looking small businesses close. They ran out of cash, not profit.

Understanding both — where the cash actually is and where the money on paper is — gives you the full picture. You cannot manage a business well without both.

## Part 2: Budgeting for Profit

A budget is a plan for money. Not a wish — a plan. It states what revenues you expect, what expenses you will incur, and what the difference is. Without a budget, you can do everything right operationally and still find yourself wondering why there is no money.

Expenses divide into two types:

**Fixed expenses** stay roughly the same month to month regardless of business activity: phone, utilities, insurance, rent, any regular subscription or service you pay for.

**Variable expenses** change in direct response to how much business activity is happening: materials, production costs, delivery, overtime. When business is slow, these go down. When business is busy, they go up.

## Gordon's Story

Gordon ran a small woodworking shop and sold custom pieces at local craft fairs and through a handful of repeat clients. By every measure he could see, the business was doing well. He had more orders than he could fill. Customers were pleased. He had won a small award from a regional crafts association.

He had no budget and no cash flow tracking. He did not see a reason to start.

In October of his third year, he had a four-week stretch with no craft fairs scheduled and two clients who delayed their orders. His revenues dropped to near zero. But his fixed expenses — the shop lease, tools insurance, his supply account at the lumber yard — kept running. He had been spending what came in and not building a cash reserve because the books always looked fine.

He came very close to shutting down a business that was genuinely good.

After that stretch, Gordon built a simple budget and began tracking cash flow every month. He discovered that his business had a predictable seasonal pattern he had never seen because he had never looked. October and February were always slow. He used that information to build a reserve during his high months.

He has not had a cash crisis since. The business is the same. The information is different.
