# Module 8 Instructor Guide — Putting It All Together

**Retiree Business Blueprint | Mastery Track | Week 8**

*For Chaz. Not for students.*

---

## Module Overview

Module 8 is the capstone week. Students work through three practical management topics (managing people/things/money, identifying where they need help, and naming their long-term strengths), one forward-looking topic (exit strategy), and a synthesis capstone (the written action plan). The module is designed to close the course with specific, actionable commitments rather than general satisfaction.

The through-line is accountability: every lesson produces a specific written output, and the final lesson requires students to look back across all eight weeks and convert the most important insights into formal behavioral objectives.

## Lesson-by-Lesson Facilitation Notes

### Lesson 8.1 — Managing for Success

**What the lesson does:** Introduces the three management areas (people, things, money) and asks students to assess their current performance honestly in each, identify the biggest gap, and commit to a specific improvement.

**Common student patterns:**
- Students typically have one area they manage well and one they avoid. The self-rating table often produces the most useful insight in the lesson — students who rate themselves low in the money area but have also not been doing monthly budget reviews will see the connection clearly.
- Probe the "people" answers carefully. Students with no employees sometimes skip this question or answer it briefly. The people area includes customer relationships, supplier relationships, and family members involved in the business — it is almost never truly empty.
- Bernard's story is useful for students who feel they have been managing well overall. The lesson's message is not that they are doing badly — it is that every area needs a defined review process.

**Opening Module 8:** Good opening question: "Did you take the this-week action from Lesson 7.5? What happened?" This creates continuity and establishes Module 8 as accountable follow-through, not a fresh start.

---

### Lesson 8.2 — Knowing When You Need Help

**What the lesson does:** Five questions that surface where the owner is spending time on work that someone else could do — and where outside help would free capacity for the core work. Students prioritize help needs and answer whether they need help now.

**Common student patterns:**
- The most productive answers come from the time-tracking reality check rather than from preferences. If a student says they do not need help, ask: "What percentage of last week was spent on tasks that required your specific skill and judgment?" The answer is often more revealing than the initial response.
- Students who are early in the business sometimes feel it is too soon to get help. The lesson's answer: if the time spent on administrative work is preventing the business from growing, it is not too soon.
- Donna's story works well with students who have service businesses — the mirror between her situation and their own is usually clear.

---

### Lesson 8.3 — Your Long-Term Business Strengths

**What the lesson does:** Three questions address the genuine long-term strengths of the student, what will sustain them in the business over years, and how to pace growth for sustainability.

**Common student patterns:**
- The most important facilitation move in this lesson is the "external evidence" requirement for Question 1. Students who answer from self-perception rather than from observable evidence often name the wrong strengths. Push: "What do your customers say? What were you known for professionally? What does the record show?"
- The pacing question often surfaces important conversations about business ambition vs. life reality. Students who are 68 years old with specific health considerations have different sustainable pace requirements than students who are 60 and healthy. This is not a discouraging conversation — it is a practical one.
- Raymond's story is effective for students who have undervalued a capability that feels natural. Prompt: "Is there something you do for clients that you have not been counting as a strength because it feels obvious to you?"

---

### Lesson 8.4 — Your Exit Strategy

**What the lesson does:** First-draft exit strategy using the Where/Who/What framework. Students assess the current transferability and long-term viability of the business.

**Common student patterns:**
- Some students resist the exit strategy topic because they have no intention of selling. Redirect: "The discipline of asking 'would someone buy this?' is a quality check on the business you are building — not a plan to leave."
- The Who factor produces the most important and most uncomfortable answers. Students whose businesses are entirely dependent on their personal presence are building something fragile, and most of them know it. Give them space to sit with that without immediately problem-solving.
- Irene's story is the most effective entry point for the Who conversation. The lesson's framing is accurate: the changes she made improved the business in her own hands, regardless of whether she ever sold it.

### Lesson 8.5 — Your Business Action Plan

**What the lesson does:** Capstone. Students review all eight modules, identify the most important output from each, and write formal behavioral objectives (personal, profits, promotional, plus additional). The lesson ends with a commitment to where the plan will be kept and when it will be revisited.

**Common student patterns:**
- The course review table is the most underestimated exercise in the module. Students who engage with it carefully — actually looking back at their workbooks from each week — often find that Module 1 and Module 3 outputs (self-knowledge and time structure) were the most important but least acted upon. That insight alone can change what objectives they write.
- The three-part objective format (what, how, by when) is where most students need the most support. First drafts are almost always missing the "how" or the "by when." Push until all three are genuinely specific.
- Walter's story often lands the hardest in this lesson — the image of the plan on the inside of the door is simple and immediately actionable. Ask the group: "Where will you keep yours?"
- This is the end of the course. The final community thread is often the most candid and the most useful for students to read. Give it time.

**Closing the course:** The final paragraph of Lesson 8.5 ("Go build something good") is the deliberate close — simple, direct, no ceremony. No additional closing content is needed.

## Assessment Notes

Module 8 produces four written outputs: management assessment, help needs list, long-term strengths articulation, exit strategy, and the action plan (which draws from all previous work). The behavioral objectives in Lesson 8.5 are the final, most important deliverable of the entire course.

An objective without a specific "by when" is not complete. An objective without a specific "how" is not complete. If facilitating live, do not accept incomplete objectives — have students revise until all three components are genuinely specific.
