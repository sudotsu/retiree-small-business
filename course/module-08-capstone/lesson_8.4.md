# Lesson 8.4 — Your Exit Strategy

### Estimated Time: Approximately 75–80 minutes

*(Reading: 25 minutes | Exercises: 35 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

Thinking about an exit strategy at the beginning of a business feels counterintuitive. You are just getting started. The end is nowhere in view.

But the business owners who eventually get the most value out of a sale or transition are the ones who have been building toward it from the beginning — not because they were eager to leave, but because the habits and structures that make a business worth buying are the same habits and structures that make it worth running.

A business designed to run well for someone else is, by definition, a business designed to run well. One that could not function without the owner — where all the systems, all the relationships, and all the institutional knowledge live in one person's head — is worth very little on the open market and is also a fragile and exhausting thing to operate day to day.

You do not have to be planning to sell. You have to be building something that would be worth selling if you decided to. The difference in how you operate is real and consequential.

By the end of Lesson 8.4, you will have:

- A clear understanding of the three factors that determine the salability and long-term viability of a small business.
- A written first draft of your exit strategy, covering all three factors.
- An honest assessment of whether your business is currently being built in a way that would make it attractive to a buyer — and what, if anything, to change.

---

## Part 1 — The Three Factors

**The Where Factor.** Location matters differently for different businesses. A coffee shop or a physical retail business derives significant value from its location — foot traffic, visibility, proximity to a complementary customer base. An office-based consulting practice where all client meetings happen by phone or video has almost no location dependency; the business can move or be operated from anywhere.

Where matters to a buyer because it affects the continuity of the business after a sale. A business that is location-dependent is worth more if the location is good and the lease is transferable. A business that is not location-dependent is worth more in a different way — it is portable, which expands the pool of potential buyers.

Know your location dependence honestly. Build accordingly.

**The Who Factor.** If the revenue of your business depends entirely on you — your personal relationships, your reputation, your continued presence in the work — a buyer is not buying a business. They are buying a job, and one that comes with the challenge of rebuilding every client relationship from scratch after the transition. That is a significant risk, and it is reflected in the price a buyer will pay.

A business where the value is transferable — where processes are documented, where staff or contractors can carry out the core work, where customer relationships are built into the business rather than residing solely in the owner's personal network — is worth more and is also more manageable to run.

The who factor is not only about hiring. It is about documentation, training, and relationship architecture. Every time you document a process, train someone else to do something only you currently do, or introduce a customer to someone else in your business, you are increasing the transferable value of what you are building.

**The What Factor.** What is the product or service you offer, and how viable is it in the current and near-future market? Is it distinctive enough that someone else would see the same opportunity in it you saw? Will what you are selling still be relevant five or ten years from now — or is it dependent on conditions that may change?

The what factor matters because a buyer is purchasing future cash flows, not past performance. They want to know that the business has a durable reason to exist and a product or service that will continue to attract customers after the sale.

## Mini-Story — Irene and the Business That Only She Could Run

Irene had operated a catering business for nine years, specializing in personal events — milestone birthdays, retirement parties, anniversary dinners. She was excellent and well-known. Her phone rang by reputation. Her clients trusted her because they had worked with her personally, and in many cases had referred her to someone they cared about.

When a health issue in her family prompted her to think seriously about the future of the business for the first time, she asked a friend who ran a small business brokerage what it might be worth on the market.

The answer was difficult. Essentially nothing as a going concern. Every relationship was Irene's personal relationship. Every recipe was in her head or on handwritten cards nobody else could decode. There were no documented processes. The one person who had worked with her for years had been hired informally, with no agreement about what she actually did or what she would continue to do. There was no training, no system that could be transferred.

What Irene had was not a business. It was a practice — entirely dependent on her continued presence.

She spent the following year making changes: documenting her core processes and recipes, formalizing her assistant's role, introducing her assistant to the clients who knew her best, building a system that could survive a week without her running it. She was not planning to sell. She was making the business she had built more durable, more resilient, and incidentally more valuable.

Within two years, the business was something a buyer might actually want. More importantly, it was something Irene could rely on even when she could not be fully present.

## Journal Prompts

Take fifteen minutes to write full responses to these questions. Full sentences, not bullet points.

**1.** If you had to stop running your business tomorrow, what would happen to it? Could someone else carry it forward? What would they need that does not currently exist?

**2.** Irene's story describes a business built entirely around one person, which made it both difficult to transition and fragile in any unexpected circumstance. Does your business have that problem? What is the first thing you could do to reduce that dependency?

**3.** On the What factor: is what you are selling today likely to still be valuable in five years? What would need to change — in the market, in your approach, or in what you offer — for it to remain viable?

**4.** You are not required to plan to sell. But the discipline of thinking "would someone buy this?" is a useful quality check on the business you are building. What would a thoughtful buyer look for in your business that you could build toward, regardless of whether you ever actually sell?
