# Lesson 8.2 — Knowing When You Need Help

### Estimated Time: Approximately 70–75 minutes

*(Reading: 25 minutes | Exercises: 30 minutes | Journal: 15 minutes)*

---

## Lesson Overview

Every business owner has a capacity limit. There are only so many hours, only so much attention, only so many areas in which any one person can do excellent work. Beyond that limit, quality declines, details are missed, and the business develops a ceiling it cannot grow past — not because of market conditions or competition, but because the owner is trying to keep too many plates spinning alone.

This lesson is about finding your limit honestly and deciding what to do with what is on the other side of it.

The answer is not always hiring an employee. For most small businesses in this course, the answer is more often a targeted outsourcing or contractor arrangement that removes specific tasks from the owner's plate, freeing time and attention for the work the owner does best. The goal is not a larger organization — it is a better-functioning one.

By the end of Lesson 8.2, you will have:

- A clear picture of where in your business you are spending time and energy on things you should not be doing yourself.
- A prioritized list of the areas where outside help — through outsourcing or a targeted hire — would most improve your business.
- A written answer to whether you need help now, and where to start.

---

## Part 1 — What You Should Keep and What You Should Hand Off

The starting point is not a job description — it is an honest inventory of what you actually do versus what you are actually good at.

Most business owners have a zone of genuine skill: the work they chose this business for, the work that produces the value customers are paying for. And around that zone, typically, is a ring of administrative, operational, and logistical work that the business also requires — work that consumes time and energy without contributing meaningfully to the core value.

Billing, scheduling, correspondence, bookkeeping, errands, supply ordering, basic web maintenance — these tasks are necessary, but they are not why the business exists. When the owner of a skilled service business spends forty percent of their week on tasks that a competent part-time assistant could handle, the opportunity cost is significant: that is forty percent less time available for the work the business exists to do.

The key question is not "can I do this myself?" Most business owners can do nearly everything their business requires. The question is "what is the best use of my time and skill, and what would the business gain if that time and skill were more concentrated on that?"

---

## Part 2 — Outsourcing vs. Hiring

Two options for getting help:

**Outsourcing or contracting** means engaging a person or service for a specific, defined scope of work — often ongoing but without the overhead of employment. A bookkeeper who handles your monthly accounts. A virtual assistant who manages scheduling and email. A web designer who maintains your site. These arrangements are usually cost-effective for tasks that require fewer hours than a part-time employee would work, or for tasks that require specialized skills you need infrequently.

**Hiring** means bringing someone into the business on a more integrated basis — a part-time employee, a dedicated helper who works alongside you regularly. This makes sense when the need is ongoing and substantial enough to justify the relationship, and when the work requires someone who understands your business well rather than someone handling a defined deliverable.

For most businesses at this stage of the course, targeted outsourcing is the more likely answer. It requires less commitment, scales more easily, and allows you to test what actually helps before making a more significant arrangement.

## Mini-Story — Donna and the Forty Percent

Donna had been running a bookkeeping service for small local businesses for three years. Her clients paid her for her accounting expertise — the review, the reconciliations, the month-end reports, the tax-season preparation work. That was what she was good at and what her clients valued.

She tracked her time for two weeks after a particularly exhausted month and found that she was spending approximately forty percent of her working hours on tasks that had nothing to do with bookkeeping: scheduling her own appointments, sending invoices to her own clients, following up on unpaid invoices, managing her own email queue, and ordering office supplies.

Each task individually was small. Together they occupied nearly half her available time — time that could have gone toward serving more clients or delivering better work to existing ones.

Donna hired a part-time virtual assistant for twelve hours a week. The assistant handled scheduling, invoicing, follow-up, and the recurring administrative queue. The arrangement cost her less per month than the revenue she generated in a single billable day.

In the following quarter, her billable hours increased by nearly a third. She had not added clients — she had simply stopped doing the work that kept her from serving the ones she had.

## Journal Prompts

Take fifteen minutes to write full responses to these questions. Full sentences, not bullet points.

**1.** What percentage of your typical working week is currently spent on tasks that someone else could do — that do not require your specific skill or judgment? Is that percentage acceptable?

**2.** What is stopping you from getting help in the area where it would help most? Is the obstacle financial, or is it something else — perfectionism, discomfort delegating, uncertainty about what to hand off?

**3.** Donna's story involves a very simple calculation: the cost of the help versus the revenue the freed time generates. Can you make that calculation for your own business? What does it tell you?

**4.** Is there anything in your business that only you can do — that would be genuinely difficult or impossible to delegate because it requires your specific expertise or your personal relationship with the client? Is that area currently getting enough of your time?

- A) Can I afford to hire someone for this task? *(Incorrect)*

- C) Would a customer notice if this task were handled by someone else? *(Incorrect)*
- D) Is this task too important to trust to a contractor? *(Incorrect)*

- A) The business is generating consistent annual revenue above a defined threshold *(Incorrect)*

- C) The owner prefers not to manage people directly *(Incorrect)*
- D) The business has been operating for fewer than two years *(Incorrect)*
