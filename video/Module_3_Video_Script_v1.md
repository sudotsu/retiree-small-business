# Module 3 Video Script — Full Narrative (v1)

**Module:** Putting Time in Checkmate
**Instructor:** Chas (on camera)
**Target length:** ~7–7:30 minutes at 125–130 WPM
**Word count:** ~920 words

---

Think about the last week you described as busy.

If someone asked you to account for where the time actually went — not how you planned it, not how you meant it to go, but where it actually went, hour by hour — how accurate would that account be?

For most people, not very. And the gap between perceived time and actual time is almost always larger than they expect.

This is not a character flaw. It is what happens when a schedule is built by accumulation rather than by design. Things get added — obligations, habits, routines — and they stay, not because you decided they should, but because they were never actively questioned. The week fills itself. You show up inside it.

This week you stop showing up inside your schedule and start designing it.

---

Two weeks ago you mapped your internal wiring — the decision drivers and defaults that describe how you're built. Last week you built two pictures: the life you have now and the business you want to place inside it, with five objectives to close the gap.

Both of those weeks produced something real. This week is where that work gets a home.

Here is the principle that runs through every lesson this week: life fills available time the way water fills a container. Whatever space is not deliberately claimed will be claimed by something else — not by bad intent, but by the natural pressure of everything that already exists in your life. If the things you identified as most important over the last two weeks are not specifically in your schedule, they will not happen. Not because you lack commitment, but because the week will not leave room for them on its own.

The only way to change that is to see the schedule clearly, decide what belongs in it, and build one that is actually yours.

---

One thing worth saying before the lessons: this is not time management in the generic sense. If you have been through a productivity seminar or read a book about scheduling, you have probably done a version of the week-mapping exercise before. What is different here is the lens. You are not building a schedule to optimize efficiency in the abstract. You are building a schedule designed around the specific defaults and big picture goals you have already worked to understand. A generic productivity template does not know about your relationship with creativity or your commitment to the people who matter most to you. Your schedule after this week will.

---

Five lessons.

Lesson 3.1 is the honest audit. You will write out your current week in full — sleep, meals, family time, health, community, habits, everything — and you will tally where the hours are actually going. Two exercises, two schedule grids. This is the longest session of the week — plan 85 to 90 minutes and do not start it unless you have the full time. The accuracy of everything that follows depends on how honest you are in this one.

Lesson 3.2 introduces the six anchors. Three come from your Module 1 default settings — the ones that need protected time or they slowly die. Three come from your Module 2 big picture goals — the elements of the life you described that need a dedicated place in the week or they stay theoretical. I've watched people build schedules that systematically suppressed the very things that kept them functioning well — their need for creative work, their investment in a key relationship, their physical health — and the cost shows up in ways that take years to undo. The anchors prevent that. They are the fixed points your revised schedule gets built around.

Lesson 3.3 is the most uncomfortable exercise of the week, and the student page says so directly. You will name at least seven specific habits, patterns, or obligations that are consuming time in your current schedule without earning their place. Three categories: things with no real value that persist out of pure habit, real relationships or obligations that are being handled in ways that cost more time than they should, and a third that involves the guilt-based commitments many people carry without examining. None of this requires that you become ruthless with your schedule. It only requires that you ask — honestly, for each item — whether you are choosing this or just continuing it.

Lesson 3.4 is where the week's work becomes commitments. Two categories, both non-negotiable: time for your business and time for yourself. Most people assume the business time is the harder commitment to protect. In practice it is almost always the other way around. Business time has urgency behind it — clients, deadlines, the pressure of something unfinished. Personal time does not. It can always happen next week. And the cost of deferring it does not appear immediately. But it compounds, and it shows up eventually in ways that are much harder to fix than a missed workout or a skipped lunch.

Lesson 3.5 is the build. You will write the revised schedule — not a day-planner, not a minute-by-minute plan, but a master template: the floor plan of a typical week, built around your six anchors and your Lesson 3.4 commitments. One specific thing to know going in: do not revise the template the first time it conflicts with real life. Real weeks will break the structure occasionally. That is normal. Give the template four weeks before changing it. The first-change rule is what separates a schedule that lasts from one that gets abandoned the first time a Tuesday goes sideways.

---

By the end of this week your objectives from Module 2 will have a home. Your defaults and big picture goals will have protected time. And the schedule you are carrying into everything that follows will be one you built on purpose.

Download and print the Module 3 workbook before you open Lesson 3.1. Two full schedule grids are in there, and you will need both.

Then open 3.1. Have 90 minutes. Write what is actually true.
