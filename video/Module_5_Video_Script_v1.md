# Module 5 Video Script — Full Narrative (v1)

**Module:** The Art of Making Money: Budgets, Cash Flow, and the Strategy for Profit
**Instructor:** Chas (on camera)
**Target length:** ~7–7:30 minutes at 125–130 WPM
**Word count:** ~920 words

---

Andy Warhol once said that making money is art, and good business is the best art.

He was not talking about greed. He was talking about the discipline of running something well — of treating the financial side of a business with the same seriousness and skill as any other craft. The same attention that a good carpenter brings to a joint or a good cook brings to a sauce.

Most people do not bring that attention to the money side of their business. Not because they do not care, but because the financial numbers feel like something that happen to a business rather than something you actively manage. You earn what you earn, you spend what you spend, and at the end of the quarter you find out how it went.

This week, you find out how it is actually going — and how to shape it.

---

Last week you built a marketing foundation: five P's, a message, a promotional draft. That work was about your customers — what they need, how to reach them, how to make them a clear offer. This week the attention turns inward, to the financial engine that has to sustain everything else you are building.

One thing worth understanding before the lessons: there is a distinction this week will ask you to hold clearly that most small business owners — even experienced ones — blur until it causes real problems.

A business can be profitable on paper and simultaneously out of cash.

These are not the same thing, and the difference is not a technicality. It is one of the most common reasons otherwise healthy small businesses fail. Profit is a calculated concept — revenues minus expenses on paper, sometimes counting income that has not yet been collected. Cash flow is a physical fact: actual money that has landed in your account and is available to spend. When a client owes you money but has not paid, that is profit. It is not cash. Your rent, your insurance, your supply order — those come out of cash.

The lesson on cash flow this week begins with a story from Forbes: a young financial controller presenting the books proudly, showing a solid profit. The elder Mr. Forbes looked at the report and said, "I don't give a damn what your books show. Do we have any money in the bank?" That question is the subject of Lesson 5.4. It will change how you read your own numbers.

---

Before the lessons: bring your bank statements from the last three months before you open Lesson 5.1. You do not need them organized. The lesson walks through that. But having the actual numbers available before you start saves significant time mid-exercise, and without them the lesson cannot be completed honestly.

---

Five lessons.

Lesson 5.1 is the baseline — revenues and expenses organized and categorized across three months. You will see, possibly for the first time in one place, exactly where the money comes from and where it goes. Plan 85 to 90 minutes. This lesson contains a three-month grid that requires your actual numbers, not estimates. If you start it without your bank statements, you will either have to stop mid-exercise or work from memory, and both produce a less honest result than the week needs.

Lesson 5.2 reads the signals already present in your business. Seasonal shifts. Customer behavior patterns. The months that are reliably stronger or weaker, and why. You have lived inside your business long enough to know some of this intuitively — this lesson asks you to name it explicitly and understand what it means for planning. Not gut feeling. Pattern.

Lesson 5.3 takes what you know about your trends and layers in what you can predict about the next three months. Not a forecast of certainty — a disciplined assessment of probable changes. New expenses you are expecting. Revenue you anticipate gaining or losing. Decisions already made that will affect the numbers. Seeing those changes mapped at the specific months they are expected is what separates planning from guessing.

Lesson 5.4 is cash flow — the real vital sign. The distinction between what you earn and what you actually have in the bank. The timing gaps that appear even in a healthy business. Your most vulnerable cash period — the month when the gap between money coming in and money going out is widest. Susan, in this week's mini-story, had twelve active clients, strong referrals, and clear profitability on paper. In September of her second year, she could not pay herself. The business account held less than four hundred dollars. None of the causes were business problems. All three were timing problems — completely predictable once she mapped them. This lesson shows you how to map them.

Lesson 5.5 is the build. Everything from the four previous lessons comes together into a three-month working budget with a net profit row, and then into the harder question this whole week has been building toward: given an honest picture of your money, what is your strategy for making this business produce a sustainable profit? Two specific adjustments that improve your margin, and the logic behind each. Not optimism. A plan.

---

By the end of this week you will have done something most small business owners never do: seen a complete, honest picture of your business's financial health — revenues, expenses, cash timing, trends, and forward-looking changes — and built a working budget based on that picture.

Bring your bank statements. Then open 5.1.
