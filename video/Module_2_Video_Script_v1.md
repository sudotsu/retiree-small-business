# Module 2 Video Script — Full Narrative (v1)

**Module:** Your Big Picture
**Instructor:** Chas (on camera)
**Target length:** ~7–7:30 minutes at 125–130 WPM

---

The question most people want to ask at this point in a business course is: what should my business be?

That question is coming. But to answer it in a way that holds up — in a way that produces something that still fits your life a year or two from now — you need to see the full picture first. Not the business picture. The life picture. What your life is actually made of right now, and where a business belongs inside it.

That's what this week builds.

---

Last week you looked inward. Decision drivers, default settings, guardrails — the invisible mechanics of how you're wired. That work lives underneath everything else.

This week you look outward. You'll build two pictures: one for your life as it currently exists, and one for your business placed inside it. You'll learn to describe both honestly — not as you'd like them to be, but as they actually are and as you specifically want them to become. By Friday, the gap between those descriptions will be converted into five concrete, dated objectives.

Not intentions. Not a vision. Five commitments with names and deadlines attached.

---

Before the lessons, one principle worth naming directly.

Your business belongs inside your life. Not the other way around.

That sounds obvious. It is harder in practice than it sounds. I have watched capable, motivated people — people who started well, planned carefully, and genuinely knew what they were doing — allow their business to gradually displace the things that motivated them to build it in the first place. It does not happen in a single decision. It happens through a series of individually reasonable trade-offs. The morning walk disappears because something needs handling. The standing lunch gets skipped for a deadline. The unhurried evening fills up. None of those choices is a catastrophe on its own. Over a year or two they accumulate into a life that looks a great deal like the career the person retired from — minus the salary.

That is a failure of structure, not character. It is entirely preventable if you build the structure correctly from the beginning.

One question to carry into every business decision you make this week: if I add this to my business picture, what does it replace in my life's picture? If the answer is something at the center of your life — a key relationship, your health, a role that genuinely matters — the cost is likely too high. If the answer is a time-waster you already flagged as draining, the trade may be exactly right.

---

Five lessons.

Lesson 2.1 is a full inventory of your life as it currently stands: every significant person in it, the activities that actually fill your time, and the roles through which others see and know you. Plan the full time — the list goes longer than people expect, and a complete inventory is more useful than a quick one.

Lesson 2.2 is the one that surprises people. You'll describe your life — as it is now and as you want it to become — in sensory terms: flavors, colors, sounds. If that sounds unusual for a business course, it is. Here's why it works: there is a difference between knowing that your current schedule is overwhelming and being able to say it tastes like burnt coffee — functional, but consumed out of habit more than enjoyment. The second description creates a genuine desire to change it. The first just produces a to-do item. You'll go through both descriptions, and the gap between where your life is and where you want it to be becomes your real motivation for the objectives you write later in the week.

Lesson 2.3 applies the same framework to your business specifically: the people involved, the activities you will actually do, the roles you will play — described twice, present and desired. The essential question running through it: does this business picture fit inside the life picture you built in 2.1 and 2.2, or does it start to crowd out what belongs there?

Lesson 2.4 shifts from picture-building to action. You'll write five behavioral objectives using a three-part formula: a specific target, a specific behavior, and a specific person responsible. Most goal-writing fails because it uses words like "improve" and "grow" — they sound like directions but can't be measured, which means they're never honestly evaluated. The formula removes that ambiguity. The difference between "I want to grow my client base" and "I will add eight new clients in the next four months by personally following up with every referral within 24 hours" is exactly the difference this lesson teaches. By the end, each of your five objectives will be specific enough that you could hand it to someone else and they would understand precisely what you have committed to.

Lesson 2.5 adds the single element that converts a good objective into a real commitment: a due date. Think about planning a trip to see family a few states away. The intention is real, the desire is there — but without a date on the calendar, it stays in the category of "soon." The moment a date gets booked, something shifts: arrangements get made, the schedule adjusts, the trip becomes an event rather than an intention. Business objectives work the same way. This lesson walks you through evaluating each objective for importance and readiness, then assigning realistic, differentiated deadlines — not the same date for everything, and not dates you can't honestly meet.
